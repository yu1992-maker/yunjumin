HSA last-month rule: eligible on December 1, contribute the full $4,400

Nov 24, 20267 min readYunjumin Forum editors

The testing period runs to December 31, 2027. Bronze plans qualify automatically from 2026; failing the test adds a 10% tax.

If you are HSA-eligible on December 1, 2026, the IRS last-month rule treats you as eligible for the whole year, so you may contribute the full $4,400 (self-only) or $8,750 (family) rather than one-twelfth of it. The price is a testing period: you must stay eligible from December 1, 2026 through December 31, 2027. Switch to a non-qualifying plan before then and the extra contribution becomes income, plus a 10% additional tax. Since January 2026, bronze and catastrophic individual plans count as qualifying coverage, so many people are already eligible without knowing it.

This article relies on IRS Publication 969 (2025 edition, page updated April 30, 2026), Notice 2026-05, Rev. Proc. 2026-24, Publication 15-B, Notice 2004-50 and HealthCare.gov. Checked on November 24, 2026. Amounts are in US dollars.

1. Eligibility is monthly, and December 1 is the exception

Under Publication 969, you are eligible for a month if, on its first day, you meet four conditions: you have a high deductible health plan (HDHP); you have no other disallowed health coverage; you are not enrolled in Medicare; and you cannot be claimed as a dependent. Citizenship, a green card and a minimum salary are not on the list. Publication 15-B adds that there is no income limit and no earned-income requirement. Form 8889 is filed with Form 1040, 1040-SR or 1040-NR, so nonresident filers have the same line.

The ordinary limit accrues by the month, one-twelfth at a time. The last-month rule is the only shortcut: if you are eligible on the first day of the last month of your tax year (December 1 for most people), you are treated as eligible all year, with the type of coverage you hold that day.

2. New for 2026: bronze plans qualify automatically

Section 71307 of the One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act changed the definition of an HDHP. Notice 2026-05 is specific:

  • For months beginning after December 31, 2025, a bronze or catastrophic plan available as individual coverage through an Exchange is an HDHP even if it fails the deductible or out-of-pocket tests (Q&A-4).
  • The same plan bought off-Exchange also counts (Q&A-6). If the plan is not on an Exchange at all, the IRS still treats you as eligible when you have no reason to know that (Q&A-7).
  • Bronze plans sold through SHOP to small employers generally do not qualify unless they meet the ordinary HDHP tests (Q&A-8).
  • Paying the premium through an ICHRA does not disqualify the plan, but the HRA may generally reimburse premiums only (Q&A-5).

Consider a self-employed person who has held a bronze plan since January 2026. In earlier years the plan's high out-of-pocket maximum ruled out an HSA. This year all twelve months qualify, with no need for the last-month rule and no testing period. The full $4,400 can still be contributed through April 15, 2027.

The window needs care. HealthCare.gov states that open enrollment for 2027 runs from November 1 to January 15, and that enrolling by December 15 gives coverage from January 1. A new Exchange plan bought now will usually not be in force on December 1 unless you qualify for a special enrollment period. This year's shortcut mainly serves people already on bronze plans and those whose HDHP starts on or before December 1 because of a new job, marriage or similar event.

The inclusion falls in the year eligibility is lost and is computed on Form 8889, Part III; death and disability are excepted. If your tax rate is unchanged, the net cost of failure is roughly the 10% tax: about $403 for self-only and about $802 for family coverage ($8,750 less one month of $729.17, times 10%). Publication 969's own example works the same way: someone with family HDHP coverage from December 1, 2025 who contributed $8,550 and lost eligibility in June 2026 must include $7,837.50.

The decision follows. Establish whether your 2027 plan will qualify, then choose the amount. If you cannot tell yet, contribute the prorated part now and the rest before April 15, 2027, by which time the 2027 plan is known.

4. Advanced techniques

  • Open first, fill later. Publication 969 states that expenses incurred before the HSA is established are not qualified, and the last-month rule does not reach back. State law fixes the establishment date. A small deposit now sets the starting point for later bills.
  • Prefer payroll. Per Publication 15-B, contributions through a cafeteria plan are exempt from Social Security and Medicare tax; contributions you make and deduct yourself save income tax only. At the employee rates in IRS Topic 751, 6.2% plus 1.45%, the gap on $4,400 is $336.60. Ask whether December payroll can be raised.
  • Receipts do not expire. Notice 2004-50, Q&A-39, sets no time limit on reimbursement, provided the expense arose after the account was established, was not otherwise reimbursed or itemized, and you keep records. Saving receipts for years leaves the money growing tax-free.
  • Spouses need separate accounts. There is no joint HSA. If both are eligible and either has family HDHP coverage, the family limit can be divided by agreement, but each $1,000 additional amount must go into that spouse's own account.
  • After 65 the account changes character. Non-medical withdrawals no longer carry the 20% additional tax, only income tax.
  • Short of cash, use an IRA. Generally once in a lifetime, a trustee-to-trustee transfer from a traditional or Roth IRA can fund an HSA. It is neither income nor deductible, uses up the year's limit, and has its own testing period through the end of the twelfth month after the transfer month.

5. Common misconceptions

  • "Being eligible in November is enough." Only December 1 counts. Eligible in November but not in December means monthly proration.
  • "The test ends with 2026." It runs 13 months, to December 31, 2027. Moving to an ordinary silver or PPO plan on January 1, 2027 is already a failure.
  • "An FSA and an HSA work together." A general-purpose health FSA disqualifies you; limited-purpose (dental, vision) and post-deductible FSAs do not. Grace-period coverage is harmless only if the prior-year balance was zero.
  • "My spouse's plan is irrelevant." A spouse's non-HDHP family plan is fine, provided it does not cover you.
  • "Overfunding does no harm." Excess contributions bear a 6% excise tax each year until removed; withdrawing them with earnings by the filing deadline, including extensions, avoids it. Retroactive Medicare enrollment turns contributions for those months into excess.
  • "Every state follows." The California Schedule CA instructions state that California does not conform to the HSA deduction. Florida has no personal income tax, so the question does not arise.

Questions

I arrived in the US only a few months ago. Can I contribute the full amount?

The four conditions in Publication 969 do not refer to arrival dates. If you are eligible on December 1 the rule applies, with the same testing period. If you keep health coverage from another country, check whether it is "other health coverage": Publication 969 does not list foreign plans, so confirm with the insurer and a tax adviser.

Can an HSA pay for care abroad?

Qualified expenses follow section 213(d). Publication 502 states that a prescription drug bought and consumed in another country counts if it is legal in both countries; drugs mailed or carried into the US generally do not.

What happens when I leave my job?

The account is yours and moves with you. Without HDHP coverage you cannot add money, but the balance can still be spent tax-free on qualified expenses.

Reminder: the IRS and your insurer prevail. For nonresident status, retroactive Medicare or state tax questions, consult a CPA or EA.
