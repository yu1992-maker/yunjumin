Income on a US Card Application: The Age-21 Line and Accessible Income

Nov 25, 20266 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Reg Z §1026.51: from 21, a spouse's accessible income may count; under 21, only money that reaches your own account.

"Income" on a US credit card application does not mean salary. Under Regulation Z §1026.51, an applicant aged 21 or older may be credited with income from a spouse or other person if there is a "reasonable expectation of access" to it. Applicants under 21 are limited to their own income, although money a parent deposits regularly into the applicant's account counts as the applicant's own. The rule is permissive: issuers may choose to recognize independent income only.

This article draws on Regulation Z §1026.51 and its official interpretations, Regulation B §1002.6, the CFPB announcement of April 29, 2013, 18 U.S.C. §1014, IRS Publication 555 and Chase's published guidance, all consulted on November 25, 2026.

1. What the issuer is required to examine

Before opening an account or raising a limit, §1026.51(a) requires the issuer to consider whether the applicant can make the minimum payments, based on income or assets and current obligations. The rule states that issuing a card to someone with neither income nor assets would be unreasonable. Three points matter:

  • The test is the minimum payment, not the full balance. The safe harbor assumes the proposed line is fully drawn from day one, then applies the product's minimum payment formula at the non-promotional rate, with mandatory charges such as the annual fee.
  • Expected income counts. The interpretations list current or expected salary, bonuses, tips and commissions; part-time, seasonal and self-employment income; interest, dividends, retirement benefits, public assistance, alimony and child support. Savings and investments are treated as assets.
  • Verification is optional. Issuers may rely on what the applicant states, on third-party data, or on a statistically sound income model.

Before 2013 issuers could generally consider only independent income, which shut out many stay-at-home spouses. The CFPB amended the rule on April 29, 2013, for applicants regardless of marital status, citing Census data showing more than 16 million married people who do not work outside the home.

Ownership by statute chiefly means community property. The interpretations give state community property law as the example; IRS Publication 555 lists Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin. Florida and New York are not on the list.

Age is measured on the day the application is submitted. Under-21 applicants face two more constraints: a written (or electronic) application is required, and an account opened on independent income cannot get a higher limit before 21 unless independent ability is shown again or a co-obligor aged 21 or over agrees in writing to assume liability. Authorized users, who owe nothing, are outside both rules.

3. Three worked cases

Case one: an H-4 spouse, 28, not employed. The partner earns $90,000, all paid into their joint checking account. Under comment 51(a)(1)-6.i, the issuer may treat the $90,000 as the applicant's income. If pay goes to the partner's sole account and $2,000 a month is transferred across, the countable figure is $24,000, not $90,000.

Case two: an international student, 20, not employed. Parents wire $2,500 every month to the student's own account, mostly for rent and living costs. Under comment 51(b)(1)(i)-3.ii, the $30,000 a year may be counted. If the parents pay the university and landlord directly, none of it counts for a student under 21.

Case three: income and credit line. Suppose the minimum is 1% of the balance plus interest, at 24% APR. A fully used $10,000 line implies about $100 plus $200, or $300 a month. That is 6% of monthly income at $60,000 a year and 20% at $18,000. Issuers must consider at least one of debt-to-income, debt-to-assets or residual income, which is why identical scores can produce very different limits. The formula and rate here are illustrative.

Practice varies. Chase says it is "sometimes" possible to include income of a spouse, household member or partner if you have access to the funds, such as through a joint account. It also lists a regular family allowance, unemployment benefits, long-term disability insurance and retirement withdrawals. Chase notes that it does not offer student cards.

Advanced techniques

  • Fix the money path first. For a non-earning spouse or a student, regular deposits into an own or joint account are the cleanest evidence. A one-off transfer looks more like an asset than income.
  • At 20, watch the date. Age is tested on submission; family payments of your expenses become countable only from 21.
  • A new job need not wait for the first pay stub. Reasonably expected income may be considered, so a signed offer is a basis where the form asks for annual income. If the form asks for current income, give the current figure.
  • Do not omit non-taxable income. Chase's list includes disability payments, workers' compensation and child support.
  • Part-time pay cannot be discounted as such. Regulation B §1002.6(b)(5) bars discounting income because it comes from part-time work, a pension or retirement benefits, though amount and probable continuance may be weighed. Paragraph (7) permits consideration of immigration status; the official example contrasts a permanent resident with a student-visa holder.
  • Limit increases face the same test, whether requested or issuer-initiated. Chase says income updates are voluntary and ignoring the prompt carries no penalty, but a higher figure may help.

Common mistakes

  • "A spouse's income always counts." The text says an issuer may treat it as income "but is not required to do so."
  • "I can list my parents' salaries." Only what regularly reaches your account, or from 21 what is regularly spent on you, is in play.
  • "Nobody checks." Chase says lenders can verify at any time and that $50,000 on a tax return against $150,000 on an application is likely to be flagged. Under 18 U.S.C. §1014, knowingly false statements to influence FDIC-insured and other covered lenders carry up to $1,000,000 in fines and 30 years in prison.
  • "All student loans are income." Only the excess over tuition and similar charges.

FAQ

Can foreign income be listed?

The interpretations to §1026.51 do not limit income by geography, but forms and verification differ. Convert to dollars, state it accurately, keep deposit records, and follow the issuer's answer.

Does an unmarried partner's income count?

The CFPB said in 2013 that the rule applies regardless of marital status. From 21, it may be considered if the access conditions are met. Community property ownership depends on a legally recognized relationship.

What if I am declined for insufficient income?

Read the stated reasons, then ask for reconsideration and explain how funds regularly reach your account. Do not inflate the figure.
