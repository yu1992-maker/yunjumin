The 1% US Remittance Tax: Cash and Money Orders Pay, Bank Cards Do Not

Nov 23, 20266 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Since 2026 cash, money orders and cashier's checks sent abroad carry a 1% tax. Accounts, checks and cards do not. Cashing a paycheck to send it counts as cash.

Since January 1, 2026, sending money abroad from the United States costs an extra 1% federal tax if you hand the provider cash, a money order, a cashier's check or a traveler's check. Paying from a bank account, by personal check, or by debit or credit card does not trigger it. The tax turns on the payment instrument, not on citizenship, visa status or whether you hold an SSN. The easiest mistake is cashing a paycheck at the remittance counter and wiring the proceeds: the proposed regulations treat that as paying cash.

This article draws on the text of Internal Revenue Code section 4475, IRS release IR-2026-48 (April 10, 2026), proposed regulations REG-114499-25 in Internal Revenue Bulletin 2026-18 (Federal Register, April 13, 2026), Notice 2026-52, and Regulation E sections 1005.30, 1005.31 and 1005.34. Sources were checked on November 23, 2026. A Federal Register search that day showed no final regulations under section 4475; taxpayers may rely on the proposed rules in the interim if they follow them in full and consistently.

1. Which payment methods are taxed

Section 4475(a) sets the rate at 1% of the transfer, owed by the sender and collected by the provider. Section 4475(c) limits it to cash, money orders, cashier's checks and "similar physical instruments." Proposed section 49.4475-1(d)(1) adds traveler's checks and states that the list is exclusive, subject to the anti-avoidance rule.

Note the card rule. The statute exempts only cards issued in the United States, while the preamble says any debit or credit card payment falls outside the tax wherever the card was issued. Final regulations could change that.

Purpose matters too. A "sender" is a natural person acting primarily for personal, family or household purposes, so business payments are outside. A transfer is foreign only if the money is received outside every state and territory, so Puerto Rico is not foreign.

2. Worked examples: the base is what the recipient gets

Under proposed section (d)(3) the base is the total transferred to the recipient, including any provider-funded bonus, but excluding fees, state tax and the remittance tax itself. Example 1 in the proposed rules: $1,000 sent in cash, a $20 fee, a $5 promotional bonus and a $12 state tax.

Example 3 is closer to daily life: a $1,000 paycheck is cashed at the counter and $800 is sent, other facts unchanged. Even if the $4 check-cashing fee is waived, the sender is treated as paying cash, and the tax is $805 × 1% = $8.05.

Over a year, $800 a month in cash with no bonus costs $96; the same amount debited from a checking account costs nothing. A sender who pays $500 a month in cash plus a one-off $3,000 in December sends $9,000 and pays $90. The sums are modest, but there is no income threshold and no exempt amount: everything depends on the instrument.

The relief concerns providers' deposit penalties only, and requires timely deposits plus full payment of any shortfall by the Form 720 due date. It does nothing for senders: the 1% is still collected. Under section 4475(b)(3), a provider that fails to collect the tax owes it.

The tax attaches at the earlier of the provider initiating the transfer or the sender paying. A transfer paid on December 31, 2026 and received on January 2, 2027 belongs to the fourth quarter of 2026.

4. Advanced moves

  • Change the instrument, not the provider. At the same counter, a debit card or a personal check payable to the provider does not trigger the tax. If you hold a paycheck, depositing it in your own account and sending from the cleared balance produces a different result from cashing it at the counter.
  • Do not swap cash for a money order or cashier's check. The proposed rules say the source of funds behind those instruments is irrelevant.
  • Cancel within 30 minutes. Regulation E section 1005.34 requires a refund within three business days, including fees and, "to the extent not prohibited by law," taxes, if you cancel within 30 minutes of paying and the money has not been picked up or deposited. If the tax is not returned by the provider, proposed section (c)(2) says the sender may be eligible to claim a refund from the IRS; the provider may not.
  • Check "Transfer Taxes" on the receipt. Section 1005.31(b)(1)(ii) requires fees and taxes to be disclosed. If you paid by card or account and were charged 1%, ask for a correction and keep the receipt.
  • Unbanked newcomers: open an account. Treasury estimates that about 7% of immigrants would remit in cash because they lack account-based alternatives; they are the most exposed. Identification requirements vary by bank.

5. Common misconceptions

  • "It is a tax on non-citizens." Section 4475 draws no line by nationality or status. A citizen paying cash is taxed; a non-citizen paying by card is not.
  • "Paying the 1% covers my reporting." It is a transaction tax. Income tax returns, FBAR and Form 3520 are separate.
  • "Splitting transfers saves money." A flat 1% is indifferent to splitting, and proposed section (d)(4) lets the IRS recharacterize transactions with a principal purpose of avoidance. Buying a prepaid card with cash and remitting immediately is the rule's own example, even if a relative does it.
  • "No tax if the money is never collected." The tax attaches on initiation or payment. Cancellation leads to a refund process.

FAQ

Do app-based services such as Wise or Remitly trigger it?

It depends on funding. Bank account, debit and credit card payments do not; cash handed over at a location does.

What about sending to my own account abroad?

The CFPB's commentary says a sender may also be the recipient, for example moving money from a US checking account to one's own foreign account. The instrument still decides; an account debit is not taxed.

Can I credit the 1% against income tax?

The statute, proposed rules and IRS pages reviewed here provide no income tax credit for senders. Ask a CPA about your situation.
