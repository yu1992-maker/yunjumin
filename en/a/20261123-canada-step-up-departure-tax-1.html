Canada's Cost Step-Up for Immigrants and the 60-Month Departure Tax Exception

Nov 23, 20265 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Foreign shares are re-based to fair market value on arrival, so Canada taxes only later gains; leave within 60 months and assets you brought escape departure tax. T1161 threshold: $25,000.

The moment you become a Canadian tax resident, most assets you own abroad (shares, funds, jewellery, art, collections) are treated under Canadian tax law as sold and immediately repurchased at fair market value. Value on the day you arrive becomes your Canadian cost, and Canada does not tax gains that accrued before then. The reverse applies on departure: most assets are deemed disposed of at market value, the so-called departure tax, but if you lived in Canada for 60 months or less in the 10 years before leaving, the assets you brought with you are exempt.

This article is based on the text of section 128.1 of the Income Tax Act and the Canada Revenue Agency's pages on newcomers' returns, dispositions of property by emigrants and Form T1161 (CRA pages updated January 20 and September 17, 2026). Sources were checked on November 23, 2026.

1. Arrival: fair market value becomes your new cost

Paragraph 128.1(1)(b) deems an individual who becomes resident to have disposed of each property owned, at fair market value, immediately before becoming resident; paragraph 128.1(1)(c) deems the same property reacquired at that value. The CRA's newcomers page puts it this way: you are considered to have sold the property and immediately reacquired it at a cost equal to its fair market value on the date you became resident, citing shares, jewellery, paintings and collections as examples.

The statute lists four exclusions for individuals:

  • taxable Canadian property, typically Canadian real estate bought before you immigrated;
  • inventory of a business carried on in Canada;
  • Class 14.1 property of a business carried on in Canada;
  • an "excluded right or interest," mainly pensions, annuities and registered-plan interests.

Excluded property keeps its original cost. Two further points matter. You report world income only for the part of the year you are resident. And the CRA's newcomers page states that income earned abroad before you became resident is not taxed in Canada, although you may need to report up to two years of pre-arrival foreign income to qualify for benefits.

2. Worked example: Canada taxes only the post-arrival gain

You land on March 1, 2026, holding foreign-listed shares with an original cost of C$50,000 and a market value of C$120,000 on the day you arrive. You sell them in 2028 for C$150,000.

ItemAmount (C$)
Original purchase cost50,000
Fair market value on arrival (new Canadian cost)120,000
Sale price in 2028150,000
Capital gain reported in Canada30,000
Capital gain without the step-up100,000

At the current 50% inclusion rate, the taxable capital gain is C$15,000 rather than C$50,000. Conversely, if the shares were underwater on arrival, the new cost is the lower market value, and the pre-arrival loss does not come with you.

Note that this is Canada's treatment only. Whether another country recognises the new cost depends on its own law. A US citizen or green-card holder moving to Canada, for instance, keeps the original cost for US purposes, so the two countries will compute different gains and foreign tax credits need to be worked out separately.

3. Departure: deemed disposition, the 60-month exception and the forms

According to the CRA's page on dispositions of property by emigrants, most property is deemed disposed of at fair market value when you leave. The main exceptions are Canadian real or immovable property, resource and timber resource property; business property used through a permanent establishment in Canada; pensions, annuities, RRSPs, PRPPs, RRIFs, RESPs, RDSPs, TFSAs and similar plans; and the short-term resident exception, under which property you owned when you last became resident (or inherited afterwards) is not deemed disposed of if you were resident for 60 months or less in the 10 years before emigrating.

StepRule
CalculationUse Form T1243 to compute the deemed gain or loss and carry it to Schedule 3

Scenario: you arrive in 2026 and leave in 2030 after 48 months. Foreign shares you brought with you fall under the 60-month exception and trigger no departure tax. ETFs bought after arrival in a Canadian non-registered account are not covered and are deemed disposed of at their value on the departure date. TFSAs and RRSPs are outside the deemed disposition, though each has its own non-resident rules after you leave.

4. Pro tips

  • Lock in evidence on arrival day: download brokerage statements showing holdings and values on your arrival date (or the nearest trading day); for jewellery, art and collections, obtain a dated valuation from a qualified appraiser. The CRA specifically advises keeping a record of fair market value on arrival, which will be your only basis for computing gains years later.
  • Treat 60 months as a hard calendar: count from the date you last became resident. Anyone planning to leave should tally the months of residence within the 10-year window; the departure tax picture can change entirely around month 60.
  • Keep imported and newly bought assets in separate accounts: imported assets may qualify for the exception and new purchases do not. Separate accounts make T1243 and T1161 far cleaner.
  • Arrange security early for large bills: electing T1244 with federal tax above $16,500 requires security, and the CRA suggests contacting it before April 30.

5. Common misconceptions

  • "Pre-arrival foreign income is taxed in Canada": the CRA says it is not, though benefit applications may ask for up to two years of foreign income.
  • "Every asset gets a step-up": taxable Canadian property, such as Canadian real estate bought before arrival, does not; it keeps its original cost.
  • "Departure tax is only for the wealthy": it turns on unrealised gains, not income. The $25,000 T1161 threshold is measured at market value, which an ordinary investment account can exceed.
  • "You file T1135 in your first year": according to the CRA's T1135 questions and answers, individuals need not file T1135 for the year they first become resident; from the next year, the test is whether the cost of specified foreign property exceeds $100,000.

FAQ

Which exchange rate applies to the arrival-day value?

Amounts are reported in Canadian dollars, generally converted at the rate on your arrival date. See the CRA's capital gains guide T4037 and consult a tax professional for specifics.

If I leave and later return, can departure tax be reversed?

The CRA states that returning former residents who ceased residence after October 1, 1996 can elect to unwind the earlier deemed disposition for property they still own.

Is a TFSA opened after arrival subject to departure tax?

It is not deemed disposed of, but new contributions while non-resident carry penalties, so check before you leave.

Reminder: the CRA website prevails; for complex situations, consult a CPA or tax lawyer.
