The First-Year Choice for Late-2026 Arrivals: 31 Days, 75% and a Delayed Return

Nov 25, 20267 min readYunjumin Forum editors

How work-visa holders who arrive in autumn 2026 can start U.S. residency mid-year, unlocking a joint return and the $32,200 standard deduction.

A work-visa holder who first arrives in the autumn of 2026 and spends fewer than 183 days in the U.S. that year is, by default, a nonresident for the whole year. If you are present for 31 consecutive days in 2026, are present for at least 75% of the days from the start of that period to December 31 (up to five days abroad count as presence), and then meet the substantial presence test in 2027, you can make the first-year choice and start residency on the first day of that 31-day window. On its own the election saves little. Its real value is that it makes a married filer a resident at year-end, which opens the door to electing full-year residency with a spouse, filing jointly and claiming the $32,200 standard deduction. The price: you cannot file until you pass the test in 2027, so you must extend by April 15 and prepay tax as a nonresident.

Sources: IRS Publication 519, U.S. Tax Guide for Aliens (2025 filing edition, chapter 1, "Dual-Status Aliens"); the IRS substantial presence test page; the instructions for Forms 8840 and 4868; and Internal Revenue Code §7701(b). Checked November 25, 2026. The current Publication 519 uses 2025 as its example year; the years below are rolled forward one year under the same rules. Rely on the 2026 edition once it is released.

1. The default: why a late-year arrival is a nonresident all year

Under the IRS substantial presence test, you are a resident for a year only if you are present at least 31 days in that year and the sum of all days this year, one-third of last year's days and one-sixth of the days two years ago reaches 183. Someone who first lands in November 2026 has roughly 50 days at most, so the second prong fails. By default they file Form 1040-NR as a nonresident for all of 2026 and become a resident in 2027.

In a year when you do pass the test, your residency start date is generally the first day you were present that year, not the day you moved. Publication 519 gives the example of a Russian citizen who attended meetings in the U.S. from January 6 to 10, 2025, and moved on March 1. Without a separate statement, residency starts on January 6. We return to this under common mistakes.

3. Worked example: arrive November 16, home for Christmas

An H-1B worker enters on November 16, 2026, flies home on December 19, returns on January 5, 2027, and stays. The spouse arrives at the same time and has no U.S. income.

  1. 31 consecutive days: November 16 to December 16 is exactly 31 days.
  2. 75% test: November 16 to December 31 is 46 days. Actual presence, November 16 to December 18, is 33 days: 71.7%, short of 75%. Publication 519 lets up to five days abroad count as presence, giving 38 days: 82.6%, a pass. The IRS's own example, a Filipino worker who arrived November 1 with 46 days present in a 61-day window, clears the bar at 75.4%.
  3. When the 2027 test is met: 33 days in 2026 count as 11, so 172 days are needed in 2027. Living here continuously from January 5, the 172nd day is June 25, 2027. The 2026 return cannot be filed before then, so Form 4868 must go in by April 15.
  4. The difference: Without the choice, the worker is a nonresident for all of 2026, cannot file jointly, must use the married-filing-separately rate schedule and gets no standard deduction. With it, residency starts November 16 and the worker is a resident at year-end, so the couple can elect full-year residency under §6013(h), file jointly and take the $32,200 standard deduction. With $28,000 of U.S. wages in 2026, taxable income can fall to zero.

The flip side: if the worker earned $40,000 abroad in the ten months before arriving, that income must now be reported in the U.S., with relief only through the foreign tax credit on Form 1116. Households with high foreign income taxed at low local rates may come out behind, so run both versions.

4. Pro tips

  • Prepay as a nonresident while extended. Publication 519 says the Form 4868 payment should be the tax you expect to owe as a full-year nonresident, which you can estimate on Form 1040-NR. You then recompute under the first-year choice or a joint return, and any overpayment is refunded. No interest accrues and the final choice is unaffected.
  • Plan travel around the five-day allowance. The 75% threshold is a hard day count, and the five-day allowance can be spread over several trips. Count the days before booking a holiday flight.
  • Use the 10-day exclusion for an early interview trip. If you pass the test but made a short visit earlier in the year, a statement can exclude up to 10 days so residency starts on the day you actually moved, shrinking the foreign income you report as a resident. A continuous stay must be excluded in full: a 12-day visit cannot be trimmed to 10.
  • Write the statement completely. It must say you were not a resident in 2025, that you meet the test in 2027 and how many days you were present that year, give the dates of the 31-day window, and list the days abroad treated as presence. An incomplete statement risks being treated as no election at all.

5. Common mistakes

  • "Residency starts the day I moved." In a year you pass the test it starts on the first day you were present, including short early visits, unless you file a 10-day exclusion statement.
  • "I can file as a nonresident now and change later." Publication 519 states the first-year choice cannot be revoked without IRS consent. If you do not follow the procedure, you are a nonresident for the full year unless you can show by clear and convincing evidence that you took reasonable steps to comply.
  • "F-1 and J-1 days count toward the 31 days and the 75%." Days as an exempt individual, such as a qualifying student or scholar, are excluded from these counts as well.
  • "Anyone under 183 days can file Form 8840." Not if you applied for a green card or had an adjustment of status pending during the year, and a late Form 8840 forfeits the exception.
  • "The full-year resident election can be repeated." A §6013(h) election is once in a lifetime, regardless of later divorce or remarriage.

FAQ

Is the first-year choice worth it for a single person?

Rarely on its own. A dual-status single filer still cannot use the standard deduction or head-of-household rates, so the result is close to a full nonresident year, while worldwide income must be reported for the resident period and foreign account reporting may apply. It matters mainly if residency status unlocks something else you need.

Do I need to file an FBAR for 2026 after making the choice?

The choice makes you a resident for part of 2026, so foreign account reporting depends on that period and your balances. See our FBAR and Form 8938 guide and consult a CPA for complex cases.

What if I still have not passed the test by October 15, 2027?

Passing the test in the following year is a precondition. If plans change and you never do, the choice is unavailable and you file 2026 as a nonresident.
