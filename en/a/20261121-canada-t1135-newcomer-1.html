Canada's Form T1135: The Newcomer Exemption and the $100,000 Cost Test

Nov 21, 20264 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Residents holding specified foreign property costing over $100,000 must file T1135. The year you arrive is exempt and cost resets to market value on arrival. US shares at a Canadian broker count; RRSP and TFSA holdings do not.

a Canadian tax resident whose specified foreign property has a total cost above $100,000 at any time in the year must file Form T1135 with the return. Newcomers are exempt for the year they become resident; thereafter cost is the fair market value on the day they arrived. The test is cost, not market value, and US shares held at a Canadian brokerage count.

Compiled from three Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) pages: "Questions and answers about Form T1135", "Foreign Income Verification Statement" and "Table of penalties", checked on 21 November 2026. The official pages prevail.

What counts and what does not

Under subsection 233.3(1) of the Income Tax Act, as summarised by the CRA, specified foreign property includes funds held outside Canada, shares of non-resident corporations (whether or not held through a broker), Canadian shares held outside Canada, debts owed by non-residents, foreign insurance policies, precious metals and futures held abroad, and non-income-producing property such as vacant land.

The CRA adds that T1135 is due by that date even if no income tax return is otherwise required.

Worked example: a 2025 arrival

A newcomer becomes resident on 1 June 2025, holding Chinese bank deposits worth CAD 70,000 and a US brokerage account worth CAD 45,000 on that day.

  • Tax year 2025: the year of arrival, so no T1135.
  • Tax year 2026: cost, measured at arrival values, totals CAD 115,000, above $100,000. T1135 is due with the 2026 return by 30 April 2027. The CRA's own example is identical in logic: $75,000 of foreign shares plus a $35,000 US bank account make $110,000, so the form is required although neither item exceeds $100,000.
  • Because cost stays below $250,000 all year, Part A, the simplified method, is available: tick the asset categories and give the top three countries. Reaching $250,000 at any point requires Part B, property by property.
  • Selling the US shares mid-year does not remove the obligation: if the threshold was exceeded at any time, every specified foreign property held during the year is reported.

The cost of lateness, per the CRA table: $25 a day for up to 100 days, minimum $100, maximum $2,500. If the failure is knowing or grossly negligent, $500 a month for up to 24 months (maximum $12,000), after which it becomes 5% of the property's cost. In this example, filing 100 days late costs $2,500.

Pro tips

  • Keep evidence of values on arrival day. That valuation sets your cost for years. Archive bank and brokerage statements dated on or nearest to it.
  • Aggregate holdings at Canadian dealers. Foreign securities held with a Canadian registered securities dealer or trust company may be reported in total, country by country, in Category 7.
  • Registered accounts sit outside the regime. The same US shares are excluded inside an RRSP or TFSA and included in a taxable account.
  • Choose Part A or Part B, not both. Between $100,000 and $250,000 either is allowed, but Part B must be completed in full.
  • Remedies exist. Omissions may qualify for the Voluntary Disclosures Program, and penalties may be relieved via Form RC4288, case by case.

Common misconceptions

  • "It is based on market value." The CRA says cost amount, generally adjusted cost base.
  • "Only the year-end balance matters." Any time in the year counts.
  • "US shares bought through a Canadian broker are not foreign." They are.
  • "Under $100,000 means no tax." The threshold only decides whether T1135 is filed; foreign income is taxable regardless of amount.
  • "Late filing only means a fine." If foreign income is also unreported and T1135 was missing, late or inaccurate, the reassessment period is extended by three years (2013 and later).

FAQ

How is cost set for foreign property received as a gift or inheritance?

At its fair market value when received, according to the CRA.

If I emigrate mid-year, must I report for the whole year?

No. The CRA requires information only for the period of residence; you may treat the departure date as the year-end.

My notice of assessment reminds me to file T1135. Did the CRA not receive it?

Not necessarily. The reminder appears automatically whenever the return indicates foreign property over $100,000.
