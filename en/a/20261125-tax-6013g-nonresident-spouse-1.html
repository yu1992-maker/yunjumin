The 6013(g) Election: A $32,200 Deduction, Once in a Lifetime

Nov 25, 20266 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Joint, separate or head of household with a nonresident spouse: the break-even, FBAR, NIIT and revocation rules.

A US citizen, green card holder or tax resident whose spouse is a nonresident alien files separately by default, with a 2026 standard deduction of $16,100. Electing under IRC 6013(g) to treat that spouse as a resident allows a joint return and a $32,200 deduction. The price is US tax on the spouse's worldwide income, and once the election is terminated neither spouse can ever make it again. Couples whose foreign income is low, or already well taxed abroad, usually gain. Couples with substantial foreign income that is barely taxed locally usually lose.

Sources: the IRS Nonresident Spouse page, Publications 519 and 501 (2025 editions), 26 CFR 1.6013-6, the instructions to Forms 8960 and 8938, IRM 4.26.16, and the 2026 figures in IR-2025-103. Checked November 25, 2026.

The middle column is the one most often skipped. Publication 501 says you are considered unmarried for head of household purposes if your spouse was a nonresident alien at any time in the year and you do not elect resident treatment. The spouse is not a qualifying person, so there must be a child or other qualifying relative, and you must pay more than half the cost of the home. A parent living in the US with children while the spouse remains abroad should run the head of household numbers first. Publication 519 cautions that being "considered unmarried" here does not carry over to the earned income credit.

A neighbouring rule, 6013(h), covers dual-status filers: nonresident at the start of the year, resident at the end, married to a citizen or resident. Publication 501 says that choice lasts one year only. A 6013(g) election rolls forward automatically. Form 1040 now carries a checkbox in the Filing Status section for both, which must also be ticked in each later year a 6013(g) election remains in effect.

2. Worked example: one salary, three foreign incomes

Assume a green card holder with $120,000 of 2026 wages, no children, no itemizing, and a spouse abroad all year. Tax uses the 2026 brackets in IR-2025-103 and ignores state tax and other credits. Filing separately, taxable income is $103,900 and federal income tax about $17,570.

On the same assumptions, untaxed foreign ordinary income of about $40,100 is the break-even point. What matters is not whether the spouse earns anything but the effective rate already paid abroad.

3. The election stops at income tax

  • FBAR ignores it. IRM 4.26.16 records FinCEN's clarification that a 6013(g) or 6013(h) election is not considered in determining residency for FBAR. A spouse with no green card who fails the substantial presence test generally does not acquire an FBAR duty by filing jointly. The US spouse still reports accounts held jointly.
  • Form 8938 does not. Its instructions list a nonresident alien who elects resident treatment for a joint return as a specified individual. For joint filers living in the US the threshold is $100,000 at year end or $150,000 at any time; for separate filers, $50,000 and $75,000.
  • Payroll tax is separate. The IRS notes the spouse may still be treated as a nonresident for Social Security and Medicare withholding.
  • Gift and estate tax are unchanged. Reg. 1.6013-6 confines the election to chapters 1, 5 and 24 and a few procedural sections, so limits on gifts to a noncitizen spouse continue to apply.
  • Treaty benefits narrow. Neither spouse can generally claim benefits as a foreign resident, though an exception to a treaty's saving clause may still cover specified income.

4. Advanced points

  1. It can be made retroactively. Publication 519 allows the election on Form 1040-X within three years of filing the original return or two years of paying the tax, whichever is later. For a 2023 return filed on April 15, 2024, that runs to about April 15, 2027. Every later return already filed must be amended as well, so total the years before starting.
  2. The net investment income tax has its own election. Per the Form 8960 instructions, 6013(g) couples default to separate treatment for the 3.8% tax: the US spouse counts only their own income against $125,000. They may elect joint treatment with a $250,000 threshold, at the cost of counting the spouse's investment income, in the first year the US taxpayer is subject to the tax. Where the spouse's portfolio is large, the default is often better.
  3. Only the first year must be joint. Later years may be separate without revoking the election, but the spouse remains a resident taxed on worldwide income.
  4. Think before revoking. Revocation, death, legal separation and inadequate records all end the election, and the IRS calls it once in a lifetime, even after remarriage. If neither spouse is a US person during a year, the election is merely suspended and revives later.
  5. The child tax credit needs one SSN between the spouses. For 2026 the IRS requires a work-valid SSN for either spouse on a joint return and for each child, for up to $2,200 per child. The EITC requires SSNs for both.

The revocation deadline is worded two ways: Publication 519 says by the due date of the return, while Reg. 1.6013-6 cites sections 6072(a) and 6081(a), the latter covering extensions. The cautious reading is the unextended date.

6. Common misconceptions

  • "Married to a foreigner, so I file as single." Anyone married at year end chooses among separate, joint and, if eligible, head of household.
  • "My spouse has never been to the US, so their income is irrelevant." After the election wages, rent, interest and gains are all taxable; foreign funds may raise PFIC issues.
  • "It is a one-year choice." It continues automatically until revoked, ended or suspended.
  • "Filing jointly makes my spouse a US resident for everything." It affects specified parts of the income tax only, not immigration status or FBAR.

FAQ

Can we file separately now and switch later?

Yes. The Form 1040 instructions allow "NRA" in place of a tax ID for a spouse who neither has nor needs one. The election can follow on Form 1040-X within the time limit.

Does the spouse's visa type matter?

Not for eligibility. One spouse must be a citizen or resident at year end and the other a nonresident. It may matter for treaty benefits given up.

What happens on divorce?

Per Publication 519, a decree of divorce or separate maintenance ends the election from the start of that year, and neither spouse can make it again.
