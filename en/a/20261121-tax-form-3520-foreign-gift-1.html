Gifts from Parents Abroad: Form 3520, the $100,000 Threshold and a 25% Penalty

Nov 21, 20265 min readYunjumin Forum editors

US persons receiving more than $100,000 in a year from foreign individuals must file Form 3520; for foreign companies the 2026 threshold is just $20,573. Late filing costs 5% a month, up to 25%.

a US tax resident who receives gifts or bequests from foreign (non-US) parents or relatives totalling more than $100,000 in a tax year must file Part IV of Form 3520. For purported gifts from foreign corporations or partnerships the bar is far lower: $20,573 for 2026. The form carries no tax of its own, but the late-filing penalty is computed on the gift itself: 5% a month, capped at 25%.

Compiled from the IRS page "Gifts from foreign person", the Instructions for Form 3520 (revised December 2025) and 26 U.S.C. §6039F, checked on 21 November 2026. The official pages prevail.

Two thresholds, two aggregation rules

Source of the giftReporting threshold (per tax year, aggregated)ItemiseAggregate with
Nonresident alien individual or foreign estateMore than $100,000Each gift above $5,000Foreign persons you know or have reason to know are related, e.g. father and mother
Foreign corporation or foreign partnershipMore than $20,116 (2025); more than $20,573 (2026), indexed annuallyEvery gift once the threshold is met, with the donor's identityForeign persons related to that corporation or partnership

Two details are easy to miss. First, §6039F(a) itself sets a $10,000 threshold, indexed for inflation; that indexed figure is what applies to foreign corporations and partnerships, while the IRS points to Section VI of Notice 97-34 for the aggregation rules behind the $100,000 individual threshold. Second, the IRS states that it may recharacterize purported gifts from foreign corporations or partnerships.

Qualified tuition or medical expenses paid directly on your behalf are not foreign gifts, as both the IRS page and §6039F(b) make clear.

Form 3520 is filed separately from the income tax return; the instructions give the mailing address as Internal Revenue Service Center, P.O. Box 409101, Ogden, UT 84409, and stress that only a complete Form 3520 counts as timely filed.

Worked example: two transfers from parents

A green-card holder living in the US receives $60,000 from their father in China in March 2026 and $50,000 from her mother in September. Neither parent is a US person.

  • Parents are related persons under the instructions, so the gifts aggregate to $110,000, above $100,000: Part IV of Form 3520 is required.
  • Both gifts exceed $5,000, so each is listed on line 54 with date, description and amount.
  • The form is due 15 April 2027, or 15 October 2027 with a timely Form 4868.

Without an extension, filing after 15 September 2027 means five months late. At 5% a month capped at 25%, the penalty is $110,000 × 25% = $27,500. Filing on time costs nothing.

The IRS's own example: $75,000 from Abby and $40,000 from Brian, both nonresident aliens. If you know they are related, you aggregate and report; if not, neither exceeds $100,000.

Pro tips

  • Tick line 1k if you extended. The IRS specifically advises checking box 1k and entering the return's form number (e.g. "1040"); otherwise the form may be treated as late.
  • Pay tuition and medical bills directly. Qualified tuition or medical payments made by relatives abroad straight to the institution are not foreign gifts and do not count towards $100,000.
  • Personal account, not company account. Family money sent from a relative's overseas company falls under the $20,573 (2026) threshold and risks recharacterization. The IRS example combines $8,000 from a foreign company with $15,000 from its owner. A transfer from a personal account, with a short gift letter, leaves a cleaner record.
  • When no single gift exceeds $5,000. The instructions say to write "No gifts or bequests exceed $5,000" in column (b) of the first line of line 54 instead of itemising.
  • Check FBAR and Form 8938 too. Form 3520 does not replace FinCEN Form 114 or Form 8938; money still held abroad is tested against those thresholds separately.

Common misconceptions

  • "The gift is taxable." The IRS defines a foreign gift as an amount the recipient treats as a gift and excludes from gross income. Part IV is an information return. The exception to watch is a gift from a "covered expatriate", which may trigger the §2801 tax and Form 708.
  • "Each transfer is under $100,000, so nothing to file." The threshold is annual and aggregates related donors.
  • "My income tax extension covers Form 3520." Only a timely Form 4868 does, up to 15 October, with line 1k completed; the extra two months for filers abroad do not apply.
  • "Nobody will notice a late form." Where a complete Form 3520 is not filed, the instructions note that the assessment period for related items stays open until three years after the information is reported (§6501(c)(8), chiefly for the foreign-trust items in Parts I–III). The usual remedy is to file promptly with a reasonable-cause statement, ideally with a CPA's input.

FAQ

My parents are US citizens or green-card holders. Do I file Form 3520?

No. Under §6039F(b) a foreign gift comes from a person other than a US person. Large gifts between US persons are handled by the donor under US gift-tax rules.

I became a resident partway through the year. Do earlier gifts count?

The requirement applies to gifts a US person receives during the tax year. A dual-status year raises questions of which receipts fall into the resident period; a cross-border CPA should decide.

Does it apply if I only have an ITIN?

Yes, if you are a US tax resident. The obligation depends on residency, not on whether you hold an SSN or an ITIN; an ITIN filer who meets the substantial presence test is covered.
