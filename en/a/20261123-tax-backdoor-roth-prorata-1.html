Backdoor Roth: Your December 31 IRA Balance Sets the Tax Bill

Nov 23, 20265 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Roth income limits start at $153,000 for 2026. Whether a backdoor conversion is tax-free depends on line 6 of Form 8606: your year-end IRA balance.

Whether a backdoor Roth (a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution followed by a Roth conversion) is close to tax-free is decided not on the day you convert but by the combined balance of all your traditional, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs on December 31 of the conversion year, which is line 6 of Form 8606. The closer that figure is to zero, the smaller the taxable share; an old pre-tax rollover IRA can make most of the conversion taxable as ordinary income. With just over a month left in 2026, that balance can still be changed.

Sources: the IRS news release on 2026 retirement limits, the Instructions for Form 8606, Publications 590-A and 590-B (2025 editions; passages citing 2026 figures checked), and Internal Revenue Code section 408(d)(3). Retrieved November 23, 2026.

2. How the pro-rata rule works: a worked example

Part I of Form 8606 treats all your traditional IRAs as one pot. Each converted dollar is tax-free only in the ratio of your basis (after-tax nondeductible contributions) to the total. The denominator is line 6 (the December 31 value of all traditional IRAs, plus outstanding rollovers) plus the year's distributions (line 7) and conversions (line 8). The instructions state that "traditional IRA" on this form includes traditional SEP and SIMPLE IRAs.

Scenario A: A single filer earning $180,000 contributes a nondeductible $7,500 in February 2026 and converts it immediately. They also hold a pre-tax IRA rolled over from a former employer's 401(k) in 2019, worth $67,500 on December 31, 2026.

  • Denominator: $67,500 (line 6) + $7,500 (line 8) = $75,000
  • Nontaxable ratio: $7,500 / $75,000 = 10%
  • Only $750 of the conversion is tax-free; $6,750 is taxable. At a 24% federal marginal rate, that is roughly $1,620 of extra tax
  • The unused $6,750 of basis carries forward on line 14

Scenario B: The same person moves the $67,500 pre-tax balance into their current employer's 401(k) before December 31, 2026. Line 6 becomes $0, the ratio is 100%, and only the small earnings between contribution and conversion are taxable. The difference, about $1,620, comes down to one year-end transfer.

4. Pro tips

  1. Roll pre-tax IRA money into your 401(k). Under IRC section 408(d)(3)(A)(ii), an IRA rollover into an employer plan is limited to the portion that would otherwise be includible in income, so only pre-tax dollars can move and your basis stays behind. That cleanly separates the two. Your plan must accept roll-ins; confirm with the administrator.
  2. Spouses are computed separately. Form 8606 is filed per person, so one spouse's large pre-tax IRA does not affect the other's ratio. A high-income couple can each do one, $15,000 combined for 2026.
  3. Married newcomers living apart. Publication 590-A provides that a married person filing separately who did not live with their spouse at any time during the year uses the single range of $153,000–$168,000, not $0–$10,000. This matters for filers whose spouse is still abroad.
  4. Model before you convert. Conversions made in 2018 or later cannot be recharacterized (Publication 590-A). If you cannot clear the year-end balance, defer the conversion rather than trying to undo it.

5. Common mistakes

  • "I only convert the new account." The ratio aggregates all your traditional, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs, at any institution.
  • "My 401(k) balance counts too." It does not. Line 6 covers IRAs only, which is why the roll-in works.
  • "I converted in January, so a later rollover is fine." A rollover of an old 401(k) into an IRA later in the year raises the December 31 balance and pulls the January conversion into the ratio.
  • "Nobody checks Form 8606." Failing to file it for a nondeductible contribution carries a $50 penalty, and overstating nondeductible contributions $100, absent reasonable cause. The bigger cost is losing your basis record and being taxed twice later.
  • "I can withdraw right after converting." Under 59½, each conversion has its own 5-year period under Publication 590-B; withdrawing taxable converted amounts inside it can trigger the 10% additional tax.

FAQ

My income is below the Roth limit this year. Do I need the backdoor?

No. Below the phase-out you can contribute to a Roth IRA directly. The backdoor is for those whose modified AGI will exceed $168,000 (single) or $252,000 (joint). If year-end income is uncertain, contributing to a traditional IRA and deciding on conversion before filing is one approach.

How long must I wait before converting?

The IRS sets no waiting period. Some people wait for a statement; that is a personal preference, not a rule.

What if I over-contribute?

Per Publication 590-A, an excess not withdrawn with its earnings by the return due date (including extensions) incurs a 6% excise tax for each year it remains.
