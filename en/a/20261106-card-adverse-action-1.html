Card Application Denied: How to Read the Adverse Action Notice

Nov 6, 20264 min readYunjumin Forum editors

A denial triggers an adverse action notice. What the reasons, score and bureau details mean, how to claim the free 60-day report, and whether to call for reconsideration.

After a credit card denial, the issuer must send an adverse action notice, on paper or electronically. It states the principal reasons for the denial, names the credit bureau whose report was used and, in most cases, discloses a credit score. Read the reasons first, check the free report against them, then decide whether to seek reconsideration or apply elsewhere.

What the notice is and why you receive it

Two federal laws apply: the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (implemented by Regulation B) and the Fair Credit Reporting Act. A single letter can satisfy both. According to the CFPB, when a credit report influenced the decision, the lender must give you the reasons or tell you how to obtain them, identify the bureau, and tell you that you are entitled to a free report.

How many key factors?

Per Consumer Compliance Outlook, up to four key factors are disclosed; if “too many inquiries” is a factor but not in the top four, it is listed as a fifth.

Reading the common reasons

Appendix C to Regulation B includes model Form C-1, a checklist of common reasons with an “Other” field. CFPB Circular 2023-03, on AI and credit denials, stresses that lenders may not simply tick checklist items if they are not the actual principal reasons; they must state the real ones. The “Other” field is often the most informative.

  • Credit history too short, or accounts too new.
  • Too many recent hard inquiries or new accounts.
  • High debt or high card utilisation.
  • Income insufficient or unverifiable.
  • A credit freeze that prevented the issuer from pulling a report. Among recent immigrants, a thin or missing US file is also common.

Steps after receiving the letter

  1. Record the date. It starts the 60-day free-report window.
  2. Read the reasons and score. Separate history problems (too new, too thin) from behaviour problems (high balances, late payments).
  3. Request the free report from the named bureau. The CFPB confirms the 60-day right. Sources describe the request process differently, so use the phone number or website in the letter.
  4. Check every line. If you see accounts you did not open, wrong late payments or wrong addresses, dispute with both the bureau and the furnisher; the bureau must investigate and correct (CFPB).
  5. Check your application for typos, commonly in income, address or a digit of the SSN/ITIN.
  6. Decide whether to call for reconsideration. See below.

Should you call reconsideration?

Card blogs including AwardWallet and NerdWallet note that automated systems sometimes decline in error, and a human review can reverse the decision or uncover application mistakes. Common advice:

  • Understand the stated reason and prepare an explanation, for example for recent inquiries or new accounts.
  • Call promptly; some guides suggest within a month. Waiting too long may mean reapplying, with another hard inquiry.
  • Reconsideration numbers vary and change; sources disagree, so rely on the issuer's site. Some issuers, Capital One among them, are said to have no dedicated line; call the applications number or ask customer service to transfer you.
  • Under Chase's 5/24 rule (five or more new cards in 24 months), reversals are rare.
  • Outcomes vary: one blogger reported a Citi denial upheld on reconsideration because of too many inquiries.

Practical next moves

  • Avoid a burst of new applications; each one adds a hard inquiry.
  • Thin file: consider a secured card or a bank's entry-level card and build a record of on-time payments.
  • High balances: bring utilisation down before reapplying.
  • Applying elsewhere: first check whether the same reason will apply there.

FAQ

1. The letter gives no specific reason. What now?

The notice must state specific reasons or explain how to obtain them. If it does not, contact the lender as the letter directs; the CFPB says you can learn the reasons within 60 days.

2. Is it illegal that no score is shown?

Not necessarily. Disclosure is required only if a score was actually used. If the lender relied on, say, income alone, there may be no score. For compliance questions, consult an attorney.

3. Does a denial hurt my score?

The denial itself is not reported, but the hard inquiry appears on your report and usually lowers the score slightly. The effect varies by scoring model; rely on the bureau's explanation.
