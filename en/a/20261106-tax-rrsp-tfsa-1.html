Canadian RRSPs and TFSAs on a US tax return: what green-card holders and citizens must know

Nov 6, 20264 min readYunjumin Forum editors

For US filers, RRSP growth can be deferred under the treaty, TFSA income is fully taxable, and both count toward FBAR and Form 8938.

US citizens and green-card holders with a Canadian RRSP can generally defer US tax on growth inside the account under the US–Canada treaty, and since 2014 that deferral is automatic. A TFSA gets no US tax shelter: interest, dividends and capital gains must be reported each year. Both accounts count toward FBAR (FinCEN 114) and Form 8938 thresholds.

Our sources are IRS pages and tax-firm articles. We could not extract text from the IRS PDF of Rev. Proc. 2020-17, so points about it come from firm summaries and are labelled as such.

RRSP / RRIF: deferral is automatic

  • Treaty Article XVIII(7) lets US citizens and residents defer US tax on income accumulating in an RRSP or RRIF.
  • Filers once had to elect annually on Form 8891. Several sources say that after Rev. Proc. 2014-55 eligible individuals are treated as having made the election.
  • Sources differ: one Baker Tilly page still says US citizens must file Form 8891 annually. It likely predates 2014, but we could not confirm its date.
  • For past omissions, the IRS issued private letter rulings in 2010–2013 granting late elections under Treas. Reg. §301.9100-3, often where the filer had not been informed.
  • Withdrawals are generally US income. Canada may withhold tax first; Form 1116 foreign tax credits can reduce double tax. Leave the calculation to a CPA.
  • RRSP contributions generally give no US deduction.

TFSA: tax-free in Canada, taxable in the US

  1. The TFSA is outside Article XVIII(7) and Rev. Proc. 2014-55; no deferral applies.
  2. Report interest and dividends on Schedule B and capital gains on Schedule D in the year earned, not the year withdrawn.
  3. Many Canadian mutual funds and ETFs (for example from RBC, TD or iShares Canada) are PFICs, each needing Form 8621, with potentially high tax plus interest. Some firms suggest holding individual stocks instead; that is one firm's view.
  4. Whether a TFSA needs Forms 3520/3520-A is disputed. Some practitioners treat it as a foreign trust; others as a custodial account. An Andersen article argues it is not a trust for US purposes; another firm notes that as of March 2026 the IRS had issued no TFSA-specific guidance. If treated as a trust and unreported, the 3520-A penalty is 5% of trust assets (minimum $10,000) and the 3520 Part III penalty for unreported distributions is 35%. These are third-party views; confirm with a CPA.

Rev. Proc. 2020-17: relief from forms, not from tax

  • Issued 2 March 2020, it exempts qualifying foreign tax-favoured trusts from Forms 3520 and 3520-A. Firm articles most often cite Canadian RESPs and RDSPs.
  • Conditions include prior compliance with US filing obligations, reporting of contributions, income and distributions, tax exemption and annual reporting under foreign law, and contribution limits.
  • Limits quoted in firm articles: retirement trusts US$50,000 a year and US$1,000,000 lifetime; non-retirement trusts US$10,000 a year and US$200,000 lifetime.
  • It does not change how income is taxed and does not remove FBAR or Form 8938.
  • Andersen says the guidance does not cover TFSAs.

  • The IRS states that Form 8938 does not replace the FBAR; both may be required.
  • Failure to file Form 8938 carries a $10,000 penalty plus up to $10,000 per 30 days after IRS notice, up to $60,000.
  • FBAR penalties are inflation-adjusted annually; wilful violations can reach 50% of the account balance (pre-1 August 2016 figure: greater of $100,000 or 50%; check FinCEN for current amounts).
  • FBAR asks for each account's maximum balance, converted at Treasury's published rate.

Self-check

  1. List every Canadian account (RRSP, RRIF, TFSA, RESP, bank and brokerage) and its highest balance for the year.
  2. RRSP: make sure you are not still using the obsolete Form 8891 process; report withdrawals as US income.
  3. TFSA: total annual interest, dividends and realised gains; check for PFIC funds.
  4. File FBAR if the aggregate exceeds $10,000; compare Form 8938 thresholds by residence and filing status.
  5. If past years were missed, have a CPA assess the correction route before filing anything.

FAQ

Is a TFSA withdrawal taxed again in the US?

Generally the tax is due in the year income is earned, not on withdrawal. Withdrawing principal and previously reported income is not usually taxed again, provided you reported each year.

My RRSP was untouched this year. What do I report?

Deferral is automatic, so usually no income and no Form 8891. The balance still counts toward FBAR and possibly Form 8938.

I am a green-card holder, not a citizen. Does this apply?

Yes. Green-card holders are US tax residents, report worldwide income, and are covered by FBAR and Form 8938.

Verify with the IRS and FinCEN. For TFSA trust treatment, PFIC calculations and late filings, consult a CPA or lawyer experienced in US–Canada cross-border tax.
