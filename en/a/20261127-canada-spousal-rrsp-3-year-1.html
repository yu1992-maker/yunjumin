Canada's Spousal RRSP: The Three-Year Rule and the One-Day Gap

Nov 27, 20264 min readYunjumin Forum editors

The contributor deducts a spousal RRSP from their own room. Withdrawals within three calendar years are taxed back to them, so 31 December versus 2 January costs a full year.

A spousal RRSP lets the higher-earning partner contribute and deduct against their own RRSP room while the account and later withdrawals belong to the spouse. Under subsection 146(8.3) of the Income Tax Act, if the contributor paid into any spousal RRSP in the year of a withdrawal or either of the two preceding tax years, the withdrawal is taxed back to the contributor. Because the rule counts the year a contribution is paid, a contribution on 31 December 2026 and one on 2 January 2027 can be deducted for the same tax year yet start the three-year clock a full year apart.

Sources: CRA pages on spousal RRSP withdrawals, the RRSP deduction limit and important RRSP dates (all dated 17 November 2026); CRA's pension income splitting page and annual limits table; the text of section 146 of the Income Tax Act. Checked on 27 November 2026.

1. Who deducts, who owns, who pays

CRA's deduction-limit page says your RRSP deduction limit covers contributions to your own RRSP and to your spouse's or common-law partner's RRSP. The spousal plan therefore uses the contributor's room and is deducted on the contributor's return; the spouse is the annuitant.

Subsection 146(8.6) lets the spouse deduct the attributed amount, so the same dollars are not taxed twice, and 146(8.5) attributes earlier contributions first.

2. 31 December versus 2 January

The first-60-days rule lets contributions from 1 January to 1 March 2027 be deducted on the 2026 return. That decides which year you deduct. Attribution follows the wording of 146(8.3), a “premium paid by the taxpayer in the year”, which is the year the money actually moves.

3. Worked example: $10,000, three withdrawal dates

A is the higher earner in 2026 with enough room; B, the spouse, expects low income in 2028 and 2029 because of parental leave or study. A contributes $10,000 to B's spousal RRSP on 30 December 2026, deducts it for 2026, and makes no further spousal contributions.

  • B withdraws $10,000 in 2028. The 2028 window is 2026 to 2028, containing $10,000 of spousal contributions. The lesser of the two is $10,000, all of it taxed to A in 2028; B files T2205.
  • B withdraws $4,000 in 2028. The lesser of $4,000 and $10,000, so $4,000 is taxed to A.
  • B withdraws $10,000 in January 2029. The 2029 window is 2027 to 2029 and contains no spousal contributions, so B is taxed. A deducted at A's marginal rate; B pays at a low-income year's rate. That is the design.

Had A waited until 2 January 2027, the deduction would still apply to 2026, but B would need to wait until 2030.

4. Pro tips

  • Contribute past 71 if your spouse is younger. CRA allows spousal contributions until the end of the year the spouse turns 71. A contributor who can no longer fund their own RRSP but still has room can keep using it for the spousal plan.
  • A final contribution in the year of death. CRA lets the legal representative contribute to the surviving spouse's RRSP in the year of death or within 60 days after, for a deduction on the final return; 146(8.7)(a) switches off attribution in the contributor's year of death.
  • Before 65, the spousal RRSP is the splitting tool. Per CRA, RRIF and annuity payments count as eligible pension income only if the transferring spouse is 65 or older at year-end (or received them because of a spouse's death); up to 50% can then be split via a joint Form T1032. For couples retiring around 60, the spousal RRSP is how incomes are balanced before 65.
  • RRIF minimums are not attributed. CRA's withdrawal page says only spousal RRIF amounts above the year's minimum enter the attribution calculation.

5. Common misconceptions

  • “The tax shifts the moment I contribute.” Withdrawals inside the three-year window come back to the contributor.
  • “A January contribution counts as last year for attribution.” Attribution follows the payment year.
  • “It uses my spouse's room.” It uses the contributor's room; the spouse's own room is untouched.
  • “Attribution still applies after separation.” 146(8.3) does not apply while the couple live apart because of a breakdown of the relationship.
  • “Newcomers can start in year one.” The contributor usually has no room in the landing year, because room is based on prior-year Canadian earned income.

FAQ

Does attribution apply if one of us becomes a non-resident?

Under 146(8.7)(b), it does not apply if either spouse is non-resident at the time of withdrawal, but non-resident withdrawals carry their own withholding. Get cross-border advice first.

What about the Home Buyers' Plan?

The HBP has separate rules, and CRA's withdrawal page points to a dedicated page. Check that page rather than applying this article's example.

Does this cover common-law partners?

Yes. Both CRA and the Act refer to a “spouse or common-law partner”.

Verify on the CRA website. For complex situations, consult a CPA or tax lawyer.
