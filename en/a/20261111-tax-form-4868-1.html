Can You Extend Your US Tax Return? Form 4868 and Whether You Still Have to Pay

Nov 11, 20264 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Form 4868 gives an automatic six-month extension to 15 October, for filing only, not payment. How to file it, the penalties and interest, and the two-month rule for those abroad.

Yes. File Form 4868 by the April deadline (or tick the extension option when paying online) and an individual return is automatically extended by six months, to 15 October. But this extends only the time to file, not the time to pay. Tax owed must still be estimated and paid by the April deadline, or penalties and interest will apply.

Written on 11 November 2026. The 15 October extended deadline for 2025 returns has passed. If you have not filed, do so as soon as possible (see penalties below), and plan ahead for the next filing season (tax year 2026).

Who can use Form 4868

The IRS says US citizens and residents, including green card holders, can use Form 4868 to request an automatic extension for individual income tax returns (the 1040 series). The IRS page currently links to the 2025 version of the form.

Three ways to request an extension

  1. Pay online and designate the payment as an extension: when paying through IRS Direct Pay, your IRS Online Account, EFTPS, debit or credit card, or a digital wallet, indicate that the payment is for an extension. No separate form is needed. Keep the confirmation number.
  2. E-file Form 4868: through IRS Free File, e-file partner software or a tax professional. E-filing requires the prior year's AGI (adjusted gross income).
  3. Mail a paper Form 4868: the IRS says fiscal-year (non-calendar-year) taxpayers can only file on paper.

Step-by-step checklist

  1. Estimate your tax for the year (using W-2s, 1099s and last year's return).
  2. Subtract tax already paid (payroll withholding, quarterly estimated payments and so on) to arrive at the estimated balance due.
  3. Request the extension by the April deadline using one of the methods above.
  4. At the same time, pay as much of the estimated balance as you can; payment is due by the April deadline.
  5. Keep the confirmation number or the e-file acceptance notice.
  6. File the actual return by 15 October and settle any difference.

Do you still have to pay? Yes, and the sooner the better

The IRS is explicit: an extension to file is not an extension to pay. Any amount unpaid after the April deadline incurs:

ItemRate
Failure-to-pay penalty0.5% of unpaid tax for each month or part of a month, up to 25%; reduced to 0.25% a month under an approved payment plan if you filed on time
Interest7% a year for underpayments in Q4 2026 (from 1 October), compounded daily
Failure-to-file penalty5% of tax due for each month or part of a month, up to 25% (applies if you miss the extended deadline)

When both penalties apply in the same month, the failure-to-file penalty is reduced by the failure-to-pay penalty: for example, 5% becomes 4.5%, plus the 0.5% failure-to-pay penalty.

The IRS also notes a minimum penalty for returns more than 60 days late: the lesser of a set amount or 100% of the tax due. For returns due in 2025, the set amount is $510. We did not verify the figure for returns due in 2026; check the IRS page.

Living abroad: an extra two months

Under IRS Topic 304, if on the due date you meet either of the following, you get an automatic two-month extension (usually to 15 June) without filing a form:

  • You are a US citizen or resident living outside the United States and Puerto Rico, with your main place of business or post of duty also outside them; or
  • You are in military or naval service outside the United States and Puerto Rico.

Three points to note:

  • Attach a statement to your return explaining which situation applies.
  • Tax not paid by the original deadline still accrues interest.
  • If you need more time, you can also file Form 4868 and tick the box on line 8, for six months in total. Again, this extends filing only, not payment.

What about state taxes?

The IRS pages do not cover state extensions. Rules vary: some states accept the federal extension, others require a separate request. States with no personal income tax, such as Florida, are not a concern, but residents of other states should check their state tax agency's website. We have not verified each state's site; please confirm for yourself.

FAQ

Q1: I cannot afford to pay. Should I still file for an extension?

Yes. An extension avoids the 5% failure-to-file penalty, which is far heavier than the 0.5% failure-to-pay penalty. Pay what you can and apply for a payment plan for the rest; while the plan is in place, the failure-to-pay rate drops to 0.25% a month, provided you filed on time.

Q2: Do I need to wait for IRS approval?

No. If you file or pay by the deadline as required, the extension is automatic. E-filing produces an acceptance notice; if you extend by paying, the confirmation number is your proof.

Q3: Can I extend beyond 15 October?

Generally no. Apart from those living abroad or serving in the military, and taxpayers in disaster areas who may receive more time (the IRS notes that disasters can bring extra time), filing after 15 October counts as late. If you are already late, file as soon as possible, because penalties accrue monthly.
