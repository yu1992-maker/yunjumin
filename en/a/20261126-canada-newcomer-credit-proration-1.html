Canada newcomers' first return: day-count proration and the 90% rule

Nov 26, 20264 min readYunjumin Forum editors

In your landing year most federal credits are prorated by days of residence; landing on 1 Sept 2026 leaves about $5,499 of BPA unless you pass the 90% test.

In the year you become a Canadian tax resident, the federal basic personal amount (BPA) and most other non-refundable credits are prorated by days of residence ÷ 365. Someone who becomes resident on 1 September 2026 can claim only $5,499.02 of the $16,452 BPA. Two exceptions apply: about a dozen credits (medical expenses, donations, tuition, CPP/EI and others) are claimed on actual amounts for the resident period, and if Canadian-source income made up 90% or more of net world income for the non-resident part of the year, the remaining credits can be claimed in full.

Sources: CRA pages "Federal non-refundable tax credits for newcomers and emigrants" and "Completing your return for newcomers" (which use 2025 figures), the CRA indexation page and a Department of Finance report (2026 figures). Checked 26 November 2026.

1. Three kinds of credit, three methods

The CRA splits the landing year into a non-resident period and a resident period. Each credit is computed for each period and added up, but the total cannot exceed what a full-year resident could claim.

CategoryTypical itemsMethod
Actual amounts in resident periodCPP/QPP and EI contributions, Canada employment amount, medical expenses, donations, tuition, student-loan interest, pension income amount, home buyers' amount, home accessibility, adoption, digital news subscriptionsClaim what was paid or incurred while resident; no proration
Prorated by daysBPA (line 30000), age amount (line 30100), spouse amount (line 30300) and other remaining creditsFull-year amount × resident days ÷ 365
Non-resident periodCPP/QPP, EI, disability amount for self, tuition, student-loan interest, donationsOnly if you report Canadian-source income for that period; if that income is at least 90% of net world income for the period, the remaining credits are available in full

Resident days start from the entry date in the "Residence information" section on page 1 of the return. The CRA says to enter the date you established significant residential ties. In its line 30000 example, arrival on 6 May counts as 240 days, so the arrival day itself is included.

2. Worked example: same salary, different landing dates

For 2026 the maximum federal BPA is $16,452 (reduced to $14,829 for high earners), and the lowest federal rate is 14%. Assume a single filer with $50,000 of Canadian net income after landing and no other credits:

Resident fromDaysBPA claimableTax value (×14%)
1 January 2026365$16,452.00$2,303.28
6 May 2026240$10,817.75$1,514.49
1 September 2026122$5,499.02$769.86

The 1 September arrival gives up $1,533.42 of federal credit compared with a full-year resident. Provincial amounts follow their own rules and are not covered here.

Second scenario: a work-permit holder works in Canada from January to August 2026 without yet establishing residential ties. Net world income for that period is $40,000, of which $38,000 (95%) is Canadian employment income. Under CRA rules the remaining federal credits, including the BPA, can be claimed in full. Had the same $40,000 been foreign salary, Canadian-source income would be 0% and proration would apply.

3. Pro tips

  • Document your residency date. It is the date significant ties were established, not necessarily the date stamped in your passport. Keep leases, moving records, bank and provincial health-card dates. The same date drives credit proration, the step-up in cost of property owned on arrival, and the start of CCB and CGEB.
  • Time unprorated expenses after landing where possible. Donations and medical expenses are claimed on actual resident-period amounts.
  • The 90% test must be claimed. On a paper return, attach a note stating net world income in Canadian dollars for the non-resident period, split between Canadian and foreign sources. The CRA says it cannot allow the full credits without it. NETFILE software has its own prompts.
  • Report pre-arrival world income, but it is not taxed. It is used for benefit calculations and the 90% test. Foreign income after you become resident is taxable, with foreign tax credits available.
  • No SIN yet? File on time anyway. The CRA suggests a paper return with a note explaining the missing SIN; you cannot file online without one. The 2026 return and balance are due 30 April 2027.

4. Common mistakes

  • "Foreign salary earned before arrival is taxable in Canada." It is not; it is reported for information only.
  • "Everyone gets the full BPA." Not in the landing year, unless you pass the 90% test.
  • "First-year RRSP contributions are deductible." The CRA says you generally cannot deduct them in your first Canadian filing year.
  • "The CRA's worked examples give the right numbers." They use 2025 amounts ($16,129 BPA). Use $16,452 for 2026.
  • "Zero income before arrival automatically passes the 90% test." Sources differ: a tax-software community post says that from the 2024 tax year, nil world income in the non-resident period no longer meets the test. We could not find that wording on the CRA pages; check the current CRA 5013-series guide.

FAQ

My spouse is still abroad. Can I claim the spouse amount?

Yes, prorated by days and reduced by your spouse's net income for your resident period. CRA example: arrival 23 September, 100 days, $16,129 × 100/365 = $4,418.90, minus $800 of spousal income, gives $3,618.90 (2025 figures).

365 or 366 days?

The CRA uses the number of days in the year; 2026 has 365.

Are provincial credits prorated the same way?

Rules vary by province, and Quebec has its own system. Check the package for your province of residence on 31 December.

Verify on the CRA website. For cross-border income or residency questions, consult a CPA or lawyer.
