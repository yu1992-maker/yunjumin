US Credit Card Disputes: The 60-Day Written Notice and the $50/100-Mile Defense Rule

Nov 22, 20266 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Under Regulation Z, a written dispute must arrive within 60 days of the first statement; issuers have up to 90 days to resolve it. Past that window, 1026.12(c) may still apply.

Statutory dispute rights on US credit cards come from the Fair Credit Billing Act (FCBA) and its implementing rule, Regulation Z. There is one hard deadline: your written billing-error notice must reach the issuer's billing-inquiry address within 60 days after it sent the first statement showing the charge. A phone call or an in-app "Dispute" button does not by itself constitute statutory notice. Beyond 60 days, an often-overlooked fallback remains: the right to assert claims and defenses under 12 CFR 1026.12(c), subject to a $50 threshold and a same-state-or-100-miles test.

Sources: the electronic Code of Federal Regulations at 12 CFR 1026.13 (billing error resolution), 12 CFR 1026.12 (unauthorized use and claims and defenses), 12 CFR 1005.6 (debit card liability under Regulation E), the text of 15 U.S.C. §1666 and §1666i, and the CFPB's consumer Q&A, all checked on 22 November 2026. Card-network chargeback rules (Visa, Mastercard, Amex) are private rules, not law, and are mentioned only where needed.

1. Two mechanisms: statutory billing errors vs. network chargebacks

Many cardholders treat the app's dispute button as their legal right. In fact there are two layers. The first is the statutory right under the FCBA and Regulation Z, which binds the issuer to fixed deadlines and procedures. The second is the network chargeback, in which the issuer reclaims funds from the merchant's acquiring bank under rules written by Visa, Mastercard and others. Issuers usually use a chargeback to enforce your statutory dispute, but the triggers and time limits differ.

12 CFR 1026.13(a) defines seven types of billing error. The ones most relevant to everyday spending are:

  • unauthorized charges ((a)(1));
  • property or services not accepted or not delivered as agreed ((a)(3)), such as a Black Friday order that never arrives;
  • payments or credits not properly posted ((a)(4)) and the issuer's own computational errors ((a)(5));
  • a request for clarification or documentary evidence about a charge ((a)(6)).

Dissatisfaction with quality is not on the 1026.13 list. That type of dispute falls under 1026.12(c), covered in section 3.

The CFPB's page "How do I dispute a charge on my credit card bill?" says the same: in addition to calling, send a written notice within 60 calendar days to protect your rights, and you may dispute a charge even after paying it.

Worked example: a Black Friday order that never arrives

Suppose you pay $1,200 for a laptop by credit card on 27 November 2026. Your statement closes on the 10th, and the first statement showing the charge is sent on 10 December 2026. Under 1026.13(b), the written notice must arrive by 8 February 2027. If you mail it on 5 January and it arrives on 8 January, the issuer must acknowledge it by 7 February and resolve it within two billing cycles, no later than 8 April 2027. Meanwhile you may withhold the $1,200 and related interest, while paying the minimum due on everything else as usual.

3. After 60 days: claims and defenses under 1026.12(c)

12 CFR 1026.12(c), implementing 15 U.S.C. §1666i, lets a cardholder assert against the issuer all claims (other than tort claims) and defenses arising from a purchase the merchant has failed to resolve, and withhold payment of the amount of credit still outstanding for that purchase plus related charges. There is no 60-day limit, and it covers defects and misdescription that 1026.13 does not. It comes with conditions:

ConditionRequirement
Merchant firstYou must have made a good-faith attempt to resolve the dispute with the merchant (1026.12(c)(3)(i)(A))
AmountThe credit extended for the purchase exceeds $50
LocationThe transaction occurred in the same state as your current billing address or within 100 miles of it
ExceptionsThe amount and location limits do not apply if the merchant is the issuer or affiliated with it, is a franchised dealer in its products, or obtained the order through a mail solicitation in which the issuer participated (1026.12(c)(3)(ii))
CapOnly the unpaid balance can be withheld; once the purchase is paid off, this lever largely disappears

The regulation does not say where an online purchase "occurs," and issuers and courts have not treated it uniformly. We draw no conclusion here.

The implication is plain: for large online purchases and prepaid services such as flights, renovation or courses, a credit card offers materially stronger legal protection than a debit card.

5. Pro tips

  • Send it to the billing-inquiry address, not the payment address. 1026.13(b)(1) requires delivery to the address disclosed under 1026.7, usually on the back of the statement or in the terms. Use USPS Certified Mail with a return receipt to prove the delivery date.
  • File in the app and in writing. The online form starts the chargeback; the letter locks in the statutory deadline. They do not conflict.
  • Autopay users: mind three business days. Under 1026.13(d)(1), if the issuer receives the notice at least three business days before the scheduled payment date, it may not deduct the disputed amount through autopay.
  • Consider not paying the disputed amount in full on a large dispute. Because 1026.12(c) reaches only the unpaid balance, keeping the disputed amount open (while paying everything else) preserves that fallback. Weigh the interest cost yourself.
  • Re-dispute to protect your credit report. If the issuer finds no error and you dispute again in writing within the payment window, 1026.13(g)(4) requires it to report the amount as disputed and tell you whom it reported to.

6. Common misconceptions

  • "A phone call counts as a dispute." It can start the process, but statutory protection depends on written notice (CFPB).
  • "Once paid, you can't dispute." The CFPB states you can; the refund usually waits for the outcome.
  • "The 60 days run from the purchase date." They run from when the first statement showing the charge was sent.
  • "Disputing gets your card closed." 1026.13(d)(3) bars closing, restricting or accelerating an account solely because you exercised the right in good faith.
  • "Chargebacks are a legal right." Network rules can change and vary by transaction type; only what the FCBA and Regulation Z list is statutory.

FAQ

Will a dispute hurt my credit score?

Under 1026.13(d)(2), the issuer may not report the disputed amount as delinquent while it investigates. Undisputed balances you fail to pay are not protected.

Do these rules apply to ITIN cardholders?

Regulation Z protects consumer credit card accounts; the text does not distinguish between cardholders with an SSN or an ITIN.

The merchant agreed to refund me but the credit never posted. Which rule applies?

A credit not properly reflected is a billing error under 1026.13(a)(4), so the 60-day written-notice rule applies.
