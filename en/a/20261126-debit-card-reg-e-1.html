Debit card fraud: $50 in two business days, $500 after, then unlimited

Nov 26, 20266 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Regulation E's three liability tiers, the 10-business-day and 45/90-day investigation clocks, why scam-induced credential theft still counts as unauthorized, and how credit cards differ.

In the US, how much of a stolen debit card's losses you absorb depends on how fast you report, not on your bank's goodwill. Under Regulation E (12 CFR 1005.6), notify the bank within two business days of learning of the loss and your liability is capped at $50; after that, up to $500; fail to report within 60 days of the statement being sent and later unauthorized transfers can be entirely yours. Credit cards fall under Regulation Z, where the cap is always $50 and the money at risk is the bank's credit line, not your cash.

Sources: the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's published text and official interpretations of 12 CFR 1005.6 (consumer liability), 1005.11 (error resolution) and 1026.12 (unauthorized credit card use), the CFPB Electronic Fund Transfers FAQs and Ask CFPB. Checked November 26, 2026. Thanksgiving to year-end is peak card season and peak fraud season.

1. Three tiers: where debit and credit part ways

The CFPB's official interpretation of 1005.6 sets out three tiers of consumer liability for unauthorized debit transfers: up to $50, up to $500, or unlimited, and notes that more than one tier can apply at once because the periods overlap. On the credit side, 1026.12(b) caps liability at the lesser of $50 or the amount obtained before the issuer is notified.

The two-business-day clock is often misread. The official interpretation excludes the day you learn of the loss and any non-business day, and counts two full 24-hour periods regardless of branch hours. Learn of the loss at 6 p.m. on a Friday, assume Saturday is a business day and Sunday is not, and the window runs from Saturday to 11:59 p.m. on Monday.

2. Worked example: the same $700, very different outcomes

The CFPB's own example: a card is stolen on Monday, the consumer knows that day, but reports on Friday. The two-business-day window closed at midnight Wednesday, so the $500 tier applies. What you actually lose depends on when the thief struck:

TimelineWithin first two daysAfter two days, before noticeConsumer bears

The bank can only push the post-window losses onto you if it shows they would not have happened had you reported in time. The interpretation gives a starker case: a $200 unauthorized debit made without a card is free if reported within 60 days, but if a further $400 leaves on day 61 and the consumer reports on day 62, the $400 can be theirs.

3. The bank's clock: 10 business days, 45 days and provisional credit

Under 1005.11 you must give notice within 60 days after the statement showing the error is sent. The bank is then bound as follows:

Determine whether an error occurred10 business daysTransfers within 30 days of the first deposit: 20 business days

Reporting resultsWithin 3 business days of finishingErrors corrected within 1 business day
Reversing provisional creditNotice required; checks and preauthorized debits honored for 5 business daysNo overdraft fees as a result

Note the point-of-sale line: a debit card misused in a store gives the bank 90 days rather than 45. That is the hidden cost of holiday shopping on debit: even with full recovery, your money can be tied up for a quarter.

4. Is a scam "unauthorized"? Four CFPB positions

Regulation E defines an unauthorized transfer (1005.2(m)) as one initiated by someone other than the consumer without actual authority, from which the consumer receives no benefit. The CFPB's FAQs read this more broadly than many people assume:

  • Tricked into sharing credentials still counts. A caller posing as the bank who obtains your login, one-time code or debit card number and then moves money has made an unauthorized transfer, including through P2P apps. Being deceived is not the same as furnishing an access device.
  • Negligence does not raise liability. Writing the PIN on the card may be negligent under state law but does not change Reg E liability.
  • Contracts cannot waive the protection. Account terms that strip Reg E rights for sharing information with a third party are void under EFTA's anti-waiver provision, 15 U.S.C. 1693l.
  • No preconditions. Banks may not require a police report before investigating or make you contact the merchant first; the CFPB treated the latter as a violation in 2019-BCFP-0001.

The line is who initiated the payment. If you yourself send money to a scammer, the transfer was initiated by the consumer and generally falls outside the definition; recovery then depends on the bank's own policy.

5. Pro tips

  • Call first, then write, and log the time. Oral notice starts the protection; if the bank asks for written confirmation you have 10 business days, and missing it lets the bank skip provisional credit. Send it the same day.
  • Put big holiday purchases on a credit card. Both carry a $50 cap, but credit card fraud ties up credit while debit fraud ties up rent money, and POS debit disputes can run 90 days.
  • Travel or hospitalization extends the deadlines. 1005.6(b)(4) requires banks to extend the time limits to a reasonable period for extenuating circumstances. Explain and keep evidence.
  • Review statements monthly. The 60 days run from when the statement is sent, not when you read it. Balance and transaction alerts cost nothing.
  • The $50 on credit cards is often waived. Reg Z says issuers need not impose liability; if they do, they must investigate reasonably and cannot deny a claim solely because you did not supply an affidavit or police report.

6. Common mistakes

  • "The bank says I shared my PIN, so no refund." If you were deceived, the CFPB treats it as unauthorized; if you wrote it on the card, negligence still cannot increase liability.
  • "My card is still in my wallet, so timing doesn't matter." The first two tiers do not apply, but the 60-day rule does, and that is where losses become unlimited.
  • "Provisional credit means I've been paid." It can be reversed after the investigation, with notice and a five-business-day cushion.
  • "Any Zelle scam is recoverable." Scammer-initiated transfers using stolen credentials are covered; payments you initiate generally are not.

FAQ

Does the two-day clock start at the theft or when I find out?

When you learn of the loss or theft, excluding that day and non-business days. The 60-day tier runs from the statement's transmittal.

The bank gave no provisional credit after 10 business days. What now?

Check whether it relied on missing written confirmation. If not, cite 1005.11(c)(2) in writing and file a CFPB complaint.

Do prepaid and payroll cards work the same way?

This article covers checking-account debit cards and credit cards. Prepaid accounts have separate Reg E prepaid rules; read the issuer's disclosures.
