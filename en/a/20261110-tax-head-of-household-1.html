Choosing a filing status: Single, Married Filing Separately or Head of Household

Nov 10, 20265 min readYunjumin Forum editors

How to choose between Single, MFS and Head of Household under IRS rules: tests, standard deductions, pitfalls and typical immigrant-family cases.

Filing status follows your marital status on 31 December. If you are unmarried, file Single. If married, compare Married Filing Jointly first; the IRS notes most couples pay less filing jointly. Married people filing apart must use MFS. Head of Household (HOH) requires that you are unmarried or "considered unmarried", paid more than half the cost of keeping up a home, and have a qualifying person.

The five statuses

The IRS lists five statuses, summarised below:

StatusApplies when
SingleUnmarried, divorced or legally separated
Married Filing JointlyMarried (or spouse died during the year)
Married Filing SeparatelyMarried and choosing not to file jointly, or saving tax by filing apart
Head of HouseholdUnmarried (or considered unmarried), paid over half of household costs and has a qualifying person
Qualifying Surviving SpouseSpouse died within the past two years and you have a dependent child

The 2025 figures come from NerdWallet and Bankrate summaries of IRS data; the 2026 figures from the IRS inflation adjustment release. Some outlets still show the pre-adjustment $15,000 / $22,500; where sources disagree, follow the IRS. Taxpayers 65 or older or blind receive an additional amount.

Head of Household: three tests

Under IRS Publication 501 you must meet all three:

  1. Unmarried or considered unmarried (see the next section).
  2. You paid more than half the cost of keeping up a home.
  3. You have a qualifying person who lived with you for more than half the year (temporary absences such as school count as time at home).

What counts as the cost of keeping up a home

  • Included: rent, mortgage interest, property tax, home insurance, repairs, utilities and food eaten at home.
  • Excluded: clothing, education, medical care, vacations, life insurance, transportation, and the value of services you or household members provide.

Who is a qualifying person

  • Children: an unmarried qualifying child; a married child only if you can claim them as a dependent.
  • Parents: you must be able to claim the parent as a dependent. Special rule: the parent need not live with you, but you must pay more than half the cost of their main home for the year; nursing-home costs count.
  • Other relatives: must live with you more than half the year, be a listed relative and be your dependent. Someone who is a "member of household" only because they lived with you all year does not qualify.
  • Only one taxpayer may use a given person for HOH in a year. A spouse cannot be your qualifying person.

Married but "considered unmarried"

Pub. 501 lets a married person use HOH only if all five are met:

  1. You file a separate return;
  2. You paid more than half the cost of keeping up your home;
  3. Your spouse did not live in your home during the last six months of the year (temporary absences still count as living there);
  4. Your home was the main home of your child, stepchild or foster child for more than half the year;
  5. You can claim the child as a dependent (or could, but for the rules allowing the other parent to claim them).

A nonresident alien spouse, common in immigrant families

If your spouse was a nonresident alien at any time in the year (for example, living abroad and meeting neither the green card nor the substantial presence test), you are treated as unmarried unless you elect to treat the spouse as a US resident, so HOH may be available. The spouse cannot be your qualifying person; you need a qualifying child or parent. Once you make the election you are married for tax purposes and the spouse's worldwide income must be reported. We could not verify on the pages checked how to make the election or whether the spouse needs an SSN or ITIN; see Chapter 1 of IRS Publication 519 (Nonresident Spouse Treated as a Resident).

The cost of filing separately

Pub. 501 lists many restrictions, notably:

  • Tax rates are usually higher than for joint filers;
  • The child and dependent care credit is generally unavailable, and the exclusion for employer-provided dependent care benefits is capped at $2,500;
  • Education credits and the student loan interest deduction are unavailable;
  • The adoption credit or exclusion is generally unavailable;
  • Income thresholds for the child tax credit, credit for other dependents and saver's credit are halved;
  • Capital loss deduction is limited to $1,500 ($3,000 jointly);
  • If your spouse itemizes, you cannot take the standard deduction;
  • If you lived with your spouse during the year, you cannot claim the elderly or disabled credit, and up to 85% of Social Security benefits may be taxable.

Separate filing may make sense where one spouse has large medical costs, wants to limit liability for the other's taxes, or the other spouse has unpaid tax or questionable returns. Have a CPA model both options.

Quick decision path

  1. Were you married on 31 December? No: go to step 3. Yes: go to step 2.
  2. Married: model MFJ first. If your spouse was not in the home for the last six months and a qualifying child lived with you more than half the year, check "considered unmarried"; if the spouse is a nonresident alien, apply the special rule above. Otherwise compare MFJ with MFS.
  3. Unmarried: did you pay more than half the household costs and have a qualifying person such as a child or a dependent parent? Yes: HOH. No: Single.

FAQ

My parents live abroad and I send them money. Can I file HOH?

Under Pub. 501 the parent must be your dependent and you must have paid more than half the cost of their main home for the year. Dependency involves several tests, including the parent's income and residency status; remittances alone do not settle it. Check Pub. 501 or ask a CPA.

I live with my partner and we have a child. Who can file HOH?

It depends on whether the child lived in your home more than half the year, whether you paid more than half the household costs and whether the child is your dependent. Only one of you may use the same child for HOH.

What if I chose the wrong status?

You can change it with Form 1040-X (Pub. 501 lists it among related forms). Be ready to support HOH with leases, bills and records of the child's address.
