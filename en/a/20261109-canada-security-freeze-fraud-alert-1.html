Freezing a Canadian credit report: security freeze and fraud alert

Nov 9, 20263 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Availability depends on the province: Quebec allows it, and Ontario reportedly does from July 2026.

Whether you can freeze a credit report in Canada depends on your province. Quebec has allowed it since 2023; Credit Karma reports Ontario from July 1, 2026; most other provinces do not. Elsewhere, a fraud alert is the usual substitute. A fraud alert asks lenders to verify identity; a security freeze locks the report so that lenders cannot view it.

What each one is

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) distinguishes them:

  • Fraud alert (identity alert): tells lenders you may be a fraud victim and that they must contact you before approving credit.
  • Security freeze: locks the report against lenders, helping prevent accounts opened in your name. FCAC adds that availability depends on your province or territory and that you must lift it before applying for credit.

Which provinces allow a freeze

  • Quebec: the 2023 Credit Assessment Agents Act allows it; several sources agree.
  • Ontario: Credit Karma says residents may use it through Equifax Canada and TransUnion Canada from July 1, 2026. We found no official page confirming the date; check with both bureaus. Another source says TransUnion has an extra year, to July 1, 2027, also unverified.
  • British Columbia: Credit Karma says legislation is planned, without a date.
  • Others: credit reporting is regulated by province, so each must legislate. Equifax and TransUnion prevail.

CBC's 2018 report that Canada had no freeze is out of date.

Fraud alert details on which sources differ

  • Cost: NerdWallet says both bureaus are free; FCAC says identification may be requested and a fee may apply.
  • Duration: NerdWallet says six years unless cancelled; another source says 90 days. Confirm with the bureau.
  • Who may apply: some say only victims, others anyone. FCAC lists suspected fraud, a stolen wallet, documents or phone, or a burgled home or car.
  • Timing: NerdWallet, citing TransUnion, says it appears at once.

Steps

  1. Check whether your province allows a freeze (Quebec does; for Ontario, check both bureaus).
  2. Contact Equifax Canada and TransUnion Canada separately; they do not share alerts or freezes. FCAC links to their contact pages.
  3. Where a freeze is unavailable, request a fraud alert.
  4. Prepare identification, ask about cost and duration, and keep confirmation.
  5. Lift the freeze before applying for a card, loan, rental or phone contract, then restore it.
  6. If your identity was used, report it to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre (1-888-495-8501 or online), the police, your bank and card issuers.

Newcomers and cross-border residents

  • US and Canadian freezes are separate systems; one has no effect on the other.
  • With a thin new file, fraud is harder to notice. Check both free reports regularly for unfamiliar accounts and inquiries, as NerdWallet advises.
  • For mortgages, car loans or rental checks, a freeze adds a step; lift it in advance.

Other protections

  • Credit monitoring: about C$30 a month (NerdWallet), free to some bank customers. Paid monitoring does not necessarily include a freeze.
  • Identity theft insurance: NerdWallet cites Equifax from C$34.95 a month with cover up to C$1 million; home insurance may include some.
  • Free reports: available from both bureaus.

Questions

1. My province has no freeze. What now?

Request a fraud alert, check both reports regularly and follow provincial legislation.

2. Does a freeze affect my score?

The sources we found do not say; ask the bureaus. Lenders cannot view a frozen report, so lift it before applying.

3. Is one bureau enough?

No. Each has its own system and does not inform the other.

Official sources prevail; provincial law and bureau rules, fees and durations may change. For identity theft or disputes, consult a lawyer or CPA.
