The 'No Interest' Store Card Trap: Retroactive Interest and the Last-Two-Cycles Rule

Nov 23, 20266 min readYunjumin Forum editors

"No interest if paid in full" is not a 0% APR: miss by $1 and the whole period's interest comes back. How Reg Z allocates payments, and how to steer them.

The familiar store-card and financing offer, "No interest if paid in full within 12 months," is deferred interest, not a 0% introductory rate. If any balance remains when the promotion ends, even $1, all interest that accrued at the contract rate over the entire period is added at once. The only reliable defence is to spread payments over the promotional months minus one, and to confirm that any extra payment is actually applied to the deferred-interest balance.

This article draws on the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's 2017 consumer guidance, Regulation Z sections 1026.7, 1026.16, 1026.53 and 1026.55 with their official interpretations, and the CFPB's December 2015 report on the consumer credit card market. Sources were checked on November 23, 2026. Black Friday and year-end are peak season for furniture, appliance, electronics and medical financing, which is when these terms are most common.

1. Two kinds of "no interest"

The CFPB's test is simple: look for the word "if." "0% intro APR for 12 months" is an introductory rate; "No interest if paid in full in 12 months" is deferred interest. Section 1026.16(h) requires that any advertisement using "no interest" or similar language state "if paid in full" clearly and conspicuously before disclosing the deferred-interest period.

The key is the official interpretation of section 1026.55: charging accrued interest at expiry would otherwise count as a prohibited rate increase, but it is permitted when the period is six months or longer and was disclosed up front.

2. Worked example: $120 short, $504 charged

The CFPB's own example is a $400 television at a 25% contract rate, paid at $25 a month, leaving $100 after 12 months. Under a 0% intro rate you owe $100. Under deferred interest you owe $165, of which $65 is retroactive interest, and that $65 then accrues interest along with the principal.

Scale it up to a typical furniture order: a $3,000 sofa, a 12-month promotion and a 29.99% contract APR.

  • $240 a month: $2,880 paid over 12 months, $120 left. Using a simplified start-of-month balance, about $504 of interest has accrued and is charged at once, taking the balance to roughly $624.
  • $250 a month: the $3,000 is cleared in 12 payments and the roughly $487 of accrued interest is waived entirely.
  • 0% intro APR comparison: at $240 a month you owe only $120 at expiry, and interest at 29.99% starts only then.

A $10 monthly shortfall costs about $500. That is the structural risk of deferred interest: the cost is not linear but jumps on a single date.

3. Payment allocation and the last two billing cycles

The problem is less visible when one card carries both a deferred-interest balance and ordinary purchases. Section 1026.53(a) requires excess payments to go to the highest-APR balance first, while section 1026.53(b)(1) treats a deferred-interest balance as carrying a 0% rate for allocation purposes. For most of the promotion, then, extra payments go to new purchases by default, and the deferred balance barely moves.

The regulation offers two protections:

  1. Automatic tilt in the final two cycles: in the two billing cycles immediately before expiry, excess payments must go to the deferred-interest balance first (1026.53(b)(1)(i)). The official interpretation gives an example: if the promotion expires on June 15 and payments are due on the 25th, the June cycle does not count because expiry precedes its due date, so the rule applies to the April and May cycles, plus any amount paid before June 15.
  2. Consumer-directed allocation: the issuer may, but need not, honour a request to apply an excess payment entirely to the deferred balance (1026.53(b)(1)(ii)). The interpretation's example is an $800 excess payment on April 25 that the consumer asks to apply to an $800 deferred balance; the issuer may do so.

In other words, if you concentrate repayment in the final two cycles, the rule puts your money in the right place. If you overpay throughout while also making new purchases, much of the overpayment may never reach the deferred balance.

4. Pro tips

  • Divide by months minus one: pay a 12-month promotion over 11 instalments ($3,000 ÷ 11 ≈ $273) to leave one cycle of buffer for posting delays and holidays.
  • Isolate the deferred balance: use the store card for that one purchase and nothing else. With no competing balance, the 1026.53 allocation problem disappears.
  • Use the date on the statement: section 1026.7(b)(14) requires the pay-in-full date on the front of every statement. Rely on that, not on the receipt or what the salesperson said.
  • Ask in writing when unsure: the interpretation lets issuers require written requests or requests made with the payment. Confirm the issuer's policy first, follow it, and keep records.
  • Price in the retroactive risk: if the same purchase can go on a general-purpose card with a 0% intro APR, a modest fee is often easier to control than deferred interest.

5. Common misconceptions

  • "Paying the minimum keeps me safe": minimum payments are not designed to clear the balance in time. In the CFPB example, on-time payments still left $100 and triggered $65 of interest.
  • "Extra payments naturally go to the promotion": with other balances on the card, the default allocation does the opposite (section 3).
  • "Only people with poor credit get caught": according to the CFPB's 2015 report, nearly 90% of deferred-interest promotional spending came from consumers with prime or superprime scores, while payoff rates for deep-subprime consumers fell below 50% on some measures. The cost is not evenly spread.
  • "Retroactive interest applies only to what is left": it is interest accrued on the declining balance for the whole period, not interest on the remaining $120.

FAQ

Is paying it off the day before expiry good enough?

Go by the date printed on your statement and the issuer's posting cut-off. Some issuers have an informal grace practice, but the 1026.16 interpretation says advertisements need not disclose such a "courtesy period," so do not count on it.

I have already been charged deferred interest. What now?

Check the promotional terms, the pay-by date on each statement and how payments were allocated. If allocation did not follow 1026.53 or the disclosure was missing, raise it with the issuer in writing, then with the CFPB.

Is medical financing structured the same way?

Many medical and dental financing plans use deferred interest. In 2013 the CFPB issued a consent order over one such medical credit card programme, finding that some enrolled providers described the promotion in misleading ways. Ask for the written terms before you sign.

Reminder: the issuer's website and your cardholder agreement prevail; for complex situations, consult a licensed attorney or a consumer credit counsellor.
