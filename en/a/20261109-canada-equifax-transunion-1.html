When Equifax Canada and TransUnion Canada disagree: reconciling line by line

Nov 9, 20264 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Why the two Canadian credit reports differ, and how to compare them, correct both, add a statement and complain.

Equifax and TransUnion are independent companies that collect data from lenders separately, so differences are normal. Three causes are common: the lender reports to only one bureau, the two received data on different dates, or the scoring models differ. To reconcile them, compare both free reports line by line, dispute any error with each bureau, and contact the lender at the same time.

1. Why the reports differ

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) recommends checking both bureaus because each may hold different information. Drawing on FCAC and several personal finance sites, the main reasons are:

CauseExplanation
Lender reports to one bureauSome lenders report to one, some to both. One blogger's Mastercard never appeared on the Equifax report.
Different reporting datesThe same lender may report on different days, creating temporary gaps.
Different scoring modelsBoth use a 300–900 scale with proprietary algorithms; identical data can produce different scores.
Different weightsBoth consider payment history, utilization, length of history, account types, public records and hard inquiries, possibly with different weights (from personal finance blogs, not an official source).

Sources differ: one blog says Equifax looks at 81 months of history and TransUnion 84; another article says TransUnion's CreditVision model looks at the last 24 months. These figures come from unofficial sources, and we found no clear statement on either bureau's site, so do not treat them as settled. On the size of the gap, one site calls 10–30 points normal, and another cites a 28-point example (TransUnion 830 against Equifax 802). Investigate when the gap is large.

2. Reconciling, step by step

  1. Get both reports on the same day. Each bureau provides a free report every year, online, by phone or by mail. TransUnion calls it the Consumer Disclosure. The phone numbers listed by FCAC are Equifax 1-800-465-7166 and TransUnion 1-800-663-9980; confirm on the official sites before calling.
  2. Build a comparison table. One row per account: lender, type, opening date, limit, balance, last reported date, delinquency status.
  3. Mark accounts that appear on only one report. Usually the lender reports to one bureau; it is not necessarily an error.
  4. Mark conflicting data. For a different balance or status, check the reporting dates first.
  5. Check personal details and inquiries. Review spelling, addresses, employers and unfamiliar hard inquiries. An account you do not recognize may indicate fraud.
  6. Prepare evidence for each issue. Statements, payment receipts and payoff letters, as FCAC advises.

3. What to do about errors

  1. Dispute with both. If the same error is on both reports, file separately; each bureau has its own form. Equifax also says the TransUnion file must be raised with TransUnion directly.
  2. Contact the lender as well. FCAC suggests asking the lender to check its records and send the update to both bureaus, which is usually faster.
  3. Wait for the investigation. The bureau checks with the lender: confirmed errors are corrected, while information the lender says is accurate stays. FCAC's page gives no time limit; one site says about 30 days, which is a third-party estimate.
  4. If you still disagree, add a statement. A consumer statement is free. The length limit is set by the bureau and may vary by province; lenders may consider it.
  5. Escalate. The federal government does not regulate credit bureaus. Start with the bureau, then follow ISED's complaint guidance or contact the provincial consumer affairs office. If the dispute concerns a federally regulated bank, use its complaint process and the external complaints body.

5. Free scores and third-party apps

Both bureaus use a 300–900 scale. Accounts of free scores vary: several sources say Equifax offers a free score to all Canadians; for TransUnion, some say residents of Quebec and Ontario, others only Quebec. Follow the official sites. Borrowell (Equifax data) and Credit Karma (TransUnion data) show scores free, though not necessarily the model a lender uses. Checking your own score is a soft inquiry and does not affect it.

FAQ

Does a 30-point gap matter?

One site says 10–30 points is common; we found no official normal range. Comparing the contents for errors or omissions matters more.

If one bureau corrects an error, will the other follow?

No. FCAC advises disputing with both, and Equifax says the TransUnion file must be raised with TransUnion. Asking the lender to update both is the safest route.

What does a dispute cost, and how long does it take?

Third-party sources say filing with either bureau is free. FCAC's page states no time limit; one site estimates about 30 days. Check the bureaus' own sites.
