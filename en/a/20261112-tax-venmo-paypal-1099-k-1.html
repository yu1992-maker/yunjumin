Got a 1099-K from Venmo or PayPal? What Is Taxable and What Still Must Be Reported

Nov 12, 20265 min readYunjumin Forum editors

A 1099-K reports gross receipts, not taxable income. The $20,000 / 200-transaction threshold from 2025, friend-to-friend payments, items sold at a loss, and how to handle forms issued in error.

The figure on a 1099-K is the gross amount a platform processed for you; it is not your taxable income. Repayments from friends, split bills, cash gifts and second-hand items sold at a loss are not taxable in themselves. But if you received the form, you generally still need to reconcile it on your return rather than ignore it.

What a 1099-K is and who receives one now

A 1099-K is sent to you and the IRS by third-party payment platforms (Venmo, PayPal, Cash App, eBay, Etsy and others). Box 1a shows the gross payments received for the year. The IRS's position: payments on a 1099-K are not necessarily taxable, and income missing from a 1099-K may still be taxable.

On thresholds, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed on 4 July 2025, restored the third-party settlement organisation (TPSO) threshold to more than $20,000 and more than 200 transactions, retroactively; the IRS confirmed this in Fact Sheet 2025-08 on 23 October 2025. Both conditions must be met, and the threshold is not indexed for inflation.

  • Payment card processors have no minimum threshold.
  • States may set lower thresholds, so you can receive a form even below the federal one.
  • The threshold only decides whether the platform issues a form; it does not decide whether you must report. The IRS says it does not affect whether payments are taxable or whether you must file.

What counts as income and what does not

SituationGeneral treatment
Side gigs, online shop, driving, freelance jobsTaxable; usually Schedule C, with reasonable expenses deductible
Transfers from friends and family, gifts, shared rent or mealsNot taxable and should not appear on a 1099-K
Personal item sold for more than you paidTaxable gain; Form 8949 and Schedule D
Personal item sold at a lossLoss not deductible, but the reported amount must be offset
Platform error (not your payments)Ask for a correction first; otherwise offset on Schedule 1

Whether you are "in business" is judged under Publication 535: essentially, whether you are carrying on selling, renting or providing services as a business.

Steps after receiving a 1099-K

  1. Reconcile first. One person may receive several forms (say, one from Venmo and one from PayPal). Line them up against bank and order records.
  2. Classify receipts: business or service income, friends and family, personal items sold (gain or loss), platform errors.
  3. Ask the platform to correct errors. The IRS says to contact the filer listed at top left, or the payment settlement entity at bottom left, and not the IRS, which cannot correct your 1099-K. Keep the correspondence.
  4. Do not delay filing if no corrected form arrives. Use the methods below.
  5. Keep evidence: receipts, photos, order screenshots and payment notes, in case the IRS writes.

How to report

1. The form is wholly or partly in error

Per the IRS FAQ: on Schedule 1, Part I line 8z (other income) enter "Form 1099-K received in error" and the amount, then the same description and amount on Part II line 24z (other adjustments). The net effect on AGI is $0.

2. Personal item sold at a loss

The IRS example: a refrigerator bought for $1,000 and sold for $700.

  • Method A (Schedule 1): enter $700 on both line 8z and line 24z, described as "Form 1099-K Personal Item Sold at a Loss". Do not enter the $1,000 cost.
  • Method B (Form 8949): if you already file Form 8949 and Schedule D, report proceeds of $700, cost $1,000, code "L" in column (f), $300 in column (g), and $0 in column (h).

Sources differ here: the IRS FAQ labels the 8z/24z loss method for tax years 2022 and 2023. Third-party sources we found say that from tax year 2024 Schedule 1 uses a separate entry space at the top for such amounts (including personal items sold at a loss), and the Intuit community says the same, but we could not verify an official statement directly. Before filing for 2025 (in spring 2026), check that year's Schedule 1 and Form 1040 instructions, or ask your software provider or a CPA.

3. Personal item sold at a gain

Gain = sale price minus cost, reported on Form 8949 and Schedule D. If you do not know the cost, check bank or card statements or ask the original seller; see Publication 551.

4. Genuine business income

Report the full receipts on Schedule C, then deduct documented costs (inventory, shipping, platform fees). A 1099-K shows gross amounts; fees and refunds are usually not netted out.

Three common traps

  • Backup withholding: if the platform lacks a valid TIN or SSN it may withhold tax, shown in Box 4, which must be claimed on your return. An ITIN works; if the number is wrong, ask the filer to correct it.
  • Do not label friend payments as "goods and services": choose "friends and family" for repayments on Venmo or PayPal to reduce the risk of them being treated as business receipts.
  • No form does not mean nothing to report: genuine business income below $20,000 / 200 transactions is still reportable.

FAQ

I only received split-bill repayments from friends but got a 1099-K. What now?

Ask the platform to correct it and keep records. The IRS says personal transfers and gifts between friends and family are neither taxable nor reportable on a 1099-K. If no correction comes, offset it on Schedule 1 lines 8z/24z.

My 2025 receipts were under $20,000. Is the form a mistake?

Not necessarily. The federal threshold is more than $20,000 and more than 200 transactions, but your state may set a lower one, and card payments have no amount threshold. Reconcile the form either way.

Selling used items at a loss doesn't need reporting, does it?

The loss is not deductible, but the IRS already has the 1099-K, so you need an offsetting entry or Form 8949 (code L) to explain it and avoid a CP2000 notice for underreported income.

Verify on the official sites. For complex situations (multiple platforms, side businesses, multi-state or cross-border receipts) consult a CPA or attorney. This is general information, not tax or legal advice.
