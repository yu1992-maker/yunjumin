Credit card refunds that leave a credit balance: the $1, 7-business-day and 6-month rules

Nov 27, 20265 min readYunjumin Forum editors

A return can push a card into a credit balance. Ask in writing and the issuer must refund within 7 business days; stay silent and it can sit for six months.

When a return leaves your credit card with a credit balance, any amount over $1 must be refunded within seven business days of the issuer receiving your written request. If you never ask, the issuer only has to make a good-faith effort to refund once the balance has sat on the account for more than six months. The return itself also runs on a legal clock: the merchant has seven business days to send the credit, and the issuer must credit it as of a date within three business days of receipt.

Sources: Regulation Z (12 CFR 1026.11, 1026.12(e), 1026.13) and the CFPB's Official Interpretations (Supplement I); Truth in Lending Act sections 165 and 166 (15 U.S.C. 1666d, 1666e). Checked on 27 November 2026.

1. From return to refund: the regulatory timeline

The typical post-Black Friday case: the statement is already paid in full, the item goes back, and the account turns negative. Regulation Z splits this into two stages, each with its own deadline.

StepWho actsDeadlineAuthority
Merchant accepts the return and sends a credit statementMerchant (not the issuer)Within 7 business days of accepting the return12 CFR 1026.12(e)(1)
Issuer credits the accountIssuerAs of a date within 3 business days of receiving the credit statement1026.12(e)(2) and commentary

Credit balance older than 6 monthsIssuerGood-faith effort to refund by cash, cheque, money order or credit to your deposit account1026.11(a)(3)
Good-faith effort fails—No further Reg Z duty; other law governs the balanceComment 11(a)(3)-2

Two details matter. The commentary says the issuer need not actually post the refund within three business days, provided it credits the account as of a date within that window, so the online posting date may lag while interest should follow the credit date. And “business day” under 1026.2(a)(6) means a day the creditor's offices are open for substantially all business functions, so federal holidays usually do not count.

2. A worked scenario

On Black Friday, 27 November 2026, you buy a $1,200 television. The statement closes on 5 December and autopay clears the full $1,200 on 28 December. You return the set on Monday, 14 December.

  1. The merchant must transmit the credit by 23 December (seven business days).
  2. The issuer has three business days to credit it; with Christmas Day in between, you may not see it until around 29 December.
  3. Autopay still takes $1,200 on 28 December, leaving the account at −$1,200.
  4. On 4 January 2027 you send a written request by secure message or letter. The issuer must refund within seven business days, roughly by 13 January.
  5. Do nothing and the $1,200 simply absorbs future purchases. If you stop using the card, the issuer need not act until the balance is six months old, around the end of June 2027.

Asking in writing versus waiting can move your cash back by more than five months.

3. Pro tips

  • Start the clock in writing. The seven-day duty in 1026.11(a)(2) is triggered by a written request. The commentary lets issuers honour oral or electronic requests, but that is permission, not obligation. A dated secure message or letter gives you an enforceable deadline.
  • Standing orders do not work. Comment 11(a)(2)-1 says an issuer need not honour a standing request to refund any future credit balance. Ask each time.
  • New spending reduces the refund. Under comment 11(a)-2 the refund is measured when it is due, net of intervening purchases, including ones not yet on a statement. If you want the full amount back, stop using the card after you ask.
  • Clear the balance before closing or product-changing a card. Request the refund, wait for it to land, then close.
  • Store credit only? Check how the merchant treats cash customers. Under 1026.12(e)(3), a merchant that routinely gives cash refunds to cash payers must give card customers a card credit or cash, unless it disclosed a no-refund policy at the time of sale. The rule does not force merchants to accept returns and allows exchanges.
  • A missing credit is a billing error. 1026.13(a)(4) covers a statement that fails to reflect a payment or other credit properly. If the merchant confirms the refund and seven plus three business days pass with nothing posted, use the written billing-error process.

4. Common misconceptions

MisconceptionThe rule
A credit balance is sent back to my bank automaticallyWithout a request, the issuer only has to try after six months (1026.11(a)(3))
A phone call starts the seven-day clockThe statutory seven business days attach to a written request; oral requests may be honoured but need not be
Balances under $1 must be refunded too1026.11(a) covers credit balances over $1 only
If I never pay interest the issuer cannot close my cardIt cannot close it solely for lack of finance charges (1026.11(b)(1)), but may close an account with no new credit and no balance for three or more consecutive months (1026.11(b)(2))
A pending return lets me underpay this statementTiming and application of the credit depend on the posting date and your cardholder agreement; keep paying what is due to avoid late fees and interest

Rewards earned on a returned purchase are commonly reversed when the credit posts; check your card's terms.

5. FAQ

What should the written request say?

The last four digits of the account, the credit-balance amount, how you want it paid (cheque or a named deposit account) and the date. 1026.11(a)(3) lists cash, cheque, money order or credit to your deposit account.

Does this apply to debit cards?

No. Sections 1026.11 and 1026.12(e) govern credit cards and open-end credit. Debit cards fall under Regulation E, and refunds go straight back to the deposit account.

What if the issuer misses the seven business days?

Keep proof of the request date, ask the issuer for an explanation, and escalate to the CFPB if needed. The legal basis is TILA section 165 (15 U.S.C. 1666d) and Regulation Z 1026.11.
