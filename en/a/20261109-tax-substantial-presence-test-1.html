Green Card, Few Days in the US: Do You Still File? The Residency Tests Explained

Nov 9, 20264 min readYunjumin Forum editors

A valid green card usually means worldwide taxation even if you spend few days in the US. The green card test, the 183-day formula and how to leave.

If you held a valid green card at any time in the year, you are generally a US tax resident who files Form 1040 on worldwide income, however few days you spent in the country. The substantial presence test (SPT) is mainly for people without a green card. Short stays do not end your filing duty; formally giving up the card or using a treaty position does.

Two tests

Per the IRS, meeting either the green card test or the substantial presence test makes you a US tax resident for the year.

TestWhoCore rule
Green card testGreen card holdersLawful permanent resident at any time in the year (IRS Pub. 519)
SPTForeign nationals without oneAt least 31 days this year and a weighted three-year total of at least 183

Publication 519 says the status, normally established by Form I-551, continues until it is formally taken away (a final administrative or judicial removal order) or you abandon it.

How the 183 days are counted

The IRS formula:

  • days in the US this year × 1
  • days last year × 1/3
  • days the year before × 1/6

You must also have at least 31 days this year. IRS example: 120 days in each of 2023, 2024 and 2025 gives 120 + 40 + 20 = 180, below 183, so the test is not met for 2025.

Excluded days include regular commuting from Canada or Mexico, transit between two foreign places of under 24 hours, days as crew of a foreign vessel, days you could not leave because of a medical condition that arose in the US, and days as an exempt individual (certain A/G visas, F/J/M/Q students and others). Form 8843 is usually needed to exclude them.

Note: the idea that short stays avoid residency, and the closer connection exception, are for people without a green card. The IRS pages we found do not say a green card holder can escape residency by spending few days.

Common situations for green card holders

  1. Valid card, mostly abroad: still a resident; worldwide income on Form 1040.
  2. Giving it up: file Form I-407 with USCIS or a consular officer, or send a letter stating your intent. Publication 519 notes you remain a tax resident until you have proof the letter was received.
  3. Treaty tie-breaker: a green card holder claiming foreign residence under a treaty is treated as a nonresident, filing Form 1040-NR with Form 8833. It depends on the treaty and carries risk; get professional help.
  4. Year of arrival or departure: you may be a dual-status taxpayer.

Giving up the card: the expatriation tax

Long-term residents must file Form 8854. The IRS defines them as lawful permanent residents in at least 8 of the last 15 tax years ending with the year of abandonment. Partial years can count; one tax firm says years properly claimed as nonresident under a treaty may not (single source; follow the IRS instructions).

  • Filing Form 8854 does not by itself mean exit tax is due.
  • Covered expatriate status depends on net worth and average annual tax. The 2025 instructions list $206,000; a third-party article gives $211,000 for 2026, which we could not verify on an IRS page.
  • You must also certify five years of federal tax compliance. Sources say failing to file Form 8854 or to certify can make you a covered expatriate automatically.

The riskiest pattern is not filing while living abroad: residency continues, penalties may build, and the five-year test may block a clean exit.

Other filings

  • FBAR (FinCEN 114): several sources say US persons file when foreign accounts together exceed $10,000 at any time; due April 15 with an automatic extension to October 15. We found no FinCEN page specific to green card holders; check FinCEN's rules.
  • Form 8938: Publication 519 lists it among forms you may need; see the IRS page for thresholds.

As of November 9, 2026, the extended 2025 FBAR deadline (October 15, 2026) has passed. If yours is late, seek professional advice.

Checklist

  1. Was the green card valid at any time this calendar year? If yes, resident by default.
  2. Days in the US last year and the year before (reference only for cardholders).
  3. Has Form I-407 been filed, with proof of receipt?
  4. Planning a treaty position? Check the article and Form 8833.
  5. Do foreign accounts total over $10,000?
  6. Has the card been held for 8 years? If so, assess exit tax first.

FAQ

I spent only 20 days in the US. Must I file?

With a valid green card you are generally still a tax resident on worldwide income. Day counts affect only the SPT.

If the card expires and I do not renew, does the duty end?

The IRS material we found says status ends by formal loss or abandonment, not by expiry. Ask a professional.

Does giving up the card always trigger exit tax?

No. It depends on net worth, average tax and compliance, but long-term residents must file Form 8854.

Rely on the IRS and FinCEN; for abandonment, treaty positions or late filings, consult a CPA or immigration/tax attorney.
