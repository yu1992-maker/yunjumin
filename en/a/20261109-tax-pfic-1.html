Chinese Mutual Funds and US Tax: What a PFIC Is and Why It Costs So Much

Nov 9, 20263 min readYunjumin Forum editors

US taxpayers holding Chinese mutual funds may face PFIC rules: the top tax rate plus interest and Form 8621. The tests, the default method and the elections.

A PFIC (passive foreign investment company) is a foreign company that mainly earns passive income. Chinese and other foreign funds are often treated as PFICs. By default, gains are spread over past years, taxed at each year's top rate and charged interest, which is why the cost is so high. Individual A-shares are usually not PFICs, though we found no authoritative analysis of them, so ask a CPA.

How a PFIC is identified

Under the Form 8621 instructions (Rev. 12/2025), a foreign corporation is a PFIC if it meets either test:

  • Income: at least 75% of gross income is passive;
  • Assets: at least 50% of average asset value produces or is held to produce passive income.

A fund's income is mainly dividends, interest and trading gains, so it tends to qualify. Several tax firms say foreign mutual funds are generally PFICs. Chinese public funds have a different legal form, and we found no uniform authority on whether each is a PFIC; have a CPA check fund by fund.

Why it is heavy: section 1291

With no election, the excess distribution rules apply:

  • Excess distribution: the part of a year's distributions above 125% of the average of the prior three years (or holding period if shorter). Nothing is excess in the first year.
  • All gain on sale: treated as an excess distribution even if the fund never paid out.
  • Spread back: the amount is allocated across the holding period. Amounts for the current year and pre-PFIC years are ordinary income; earlier years are taxed at that year's top rate (37% for 2018 to 2025 per the instructions), plus interest.
  • Interest: under IRC section 6621.
  • No preferential capital gains rate: per several tax firms.

The longer you hold and the more you gain, the more years share the tax and the more interest builds.

Two alternatives

  1. QEF election (§ 1295): include your share of ordinary earnings as ordinary income and net capital gain as long-term gain each year. The fund must supply US-style data, which Chinese funds almost never do. Elected fund by fund.
  2. Mark to market (§ 1296): only for marketable stock regularly traded on a qualified exchange. Gains above basis are ordinary income each year; losses are deductible only to the extent of prior inclusions. We found no clear answer for over-the-counter Chinese public funds; ask a CPA.

Who files Form 8621

A US person holding a PFIC directly or indirectly files in a year when any of these applies, generally one form per PFIC:

  • a distribution is received;
  • stock is sold or redeemed with gain;
  • a QEF or mark-to-market report is made;
  • an election in Part II is required;
  • section 1298(f) reporting applies.

Exception: if total PFIC value at year end is $25,000 or less ($50,000 joint) with no excess distribution or gain, Part I can be skipped. The instructions stress it covers only Part I, not the whole form; indirect holdings of section 1291 funds have a separate $5,000 threshold.

Checklist

  1. List all domestic funds, wealth products and foreign accounts, separating funds from stocks.
  2. Convert at the year-end rate and compare with $25,000 / $50,000.
  3. Have a CPA test each holding and any election.
  4. Model the look-back tax on long holdings; think twice before buying new ones.
  5. Keep purchase dates, cost and distribution records.

FAQ

Are individual A-shares PFICs?

Operating companies generally fail both tests, but we found no authoritative discussion; holding and investment companies need separate review.

Can small holdings skip reporting?

At or below $25,000 ($50,000 joint) with no excess distribution or sale gain, Part I may be skipped. A gain or excess distribution brings the default rules.

What is the PFIC tax rate?

There is no single rate. Amounts allocated to prior years use that year's top rate, 37% for 2018 to 2025, plus interest.
