Canadian tax-loss selling: the December 30 cut-off and the 61-day zone

Nov 24, 20267 min readYunjumin Forum editors

A TFSA repurchase destroys the loss, a mistake can be undone within 30 days, and newcomers measure from landing-day value.

For a loss to count in 2026, the last trade date for Canadian- and U.S.-listed stocks and ETFs is December 30, 2026, because tax follows the settlement date rather than the order date. The date is only half of it. If you, your spouse, or either spouse's TFSA or RRSP buys the same security within 30 days on either side of the sale and still holds it at the end of the 30th day after, the loss is a superficial loss and is nil for the year. Repurchase in your own taxable account merely defers it; repurchase in a TFSA or RRSP destroys it.

This article relies on sections 39, 40, 47, 53, 54, 128.1 and 251.1 of the Income Tax Act (Justice Laws website, current to October 29, 2026), CRA guide T4037, CRA technical interpretations 2021-0896031C6 and 2015-0588981C6, and the TMX trading calendar, with supporting material from CIBC, Questrade, TaxTips and BNN Bloomberg. Checked on November 24, 2026. T4037 is still the 2025 edition. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell.

1. The calendar: two usable sessions after Christmas

In a 2015 interpretation the CRA confirmed that a share sale is a disposition on the settlement date, and that foreign-currency proceeds are converted at that day's rate. Canada moved to T+1 in May 2024, so trades settle one business day later. The TMX calendar shows an early close on December 24, 2026 and closures on December 25 and 28.

2. The 61-day zone: both conditions must hold

The section 54 definition has two limbs. First, between 30 days before and 30 days after the disposition, you or an affiliated person acquires the same or identical property. Second, at the end of that period you or an affiliated person owns it or has a right to acquire it. Paragraph 40(2)(g)(i) sets such a loss at nil, and paragraph 53(1)(f) adds the denied amount to the cost (ACB) of the substituted property.

Who is affiliated: section 251.1 covers yourself, a spouse or common-law partner, a controlled corporation, and a trust of which you are a majority-interest beneficiary. On that basis the CRA held in 2021-0896031C6 that a spouse's RRSP trust is affiliated. Adult children and parents are not covered by the rule for individuals.

How to count: the same interpretation says the day of disposition is excluded. After a September 1 disposition, October 1 is still inside the zone; October 2 is safe. On that count, a sale settling December 31, 2026 has a zone of December 1, 2026 to January 30, 2027. January 30 is a Saturday, and a right to acquire counts as ownership, so an order placed January 29 and settling February 1 is arguable. The cautious approach is to order from February 1.

3. Worked examples: how $4,000 of tax value is kept or lost

Case one. An investor has realized $20,000 of gains in 2026 and holds a stock with a $20,000 unrealized loss; assume a 40% marginal rate. With a 50% inclusion rate, full offset saves $20,000 × 50% × 40% = $4,000. Sell on December 30 and rebuy the same stock in a TFSA on January 11, and that $4,000 is not deferred. It is gone.

Case two, the automatic investor. You hold 300 ETF units, buy 10 a month automatically, and sell 100 on December 30 at a $2,500 loss. One purchase in December and one in January fall inside the zone, and 220 units remain at the end. A March 2026 CIBC paper sets out the CRA's administrative formula: denied amount = (least of S, P and B) ÷ S × loss, where S is units sold, P units bought in the zone and B units left at the end. Here 20 ÷ 100 × $2,500 = $500. So $2,000 is usable and $500 goes to ACB. Dividend reinvestment counts toward P.

Case three, the newcomer. Under paragraph 128.1(1)(c) you are deemed to reacquire property at market value on becoming resident. Shares bought at US$40 before arrival and worth US$80 on landing day at 1.40 have an ACB of C$112. Sold in December at US$78 and 1.38, proceeds are C$107.64, a loss of C$4.36 a share, or C$2,180 on 500 shares. The position nearly doubled, yet Canada sees a loss. The reverse also happens: in BNN Bloomberg's example, a stock bought at US$100 (1.30) and sold at US$95 (1.40) is down 5% in U.S. dollars but up C$3.

4. Advanced techniques

  • The loss can be rescued within 30 days. In 2021-0896031C6 a husband sold on September 1 at a $20,000 loss and his wife's RRSP bought the same stock on September 7. The CRA confirmed that if the RRSP sold by September 28, the second condition failed and the loss stood. Had it rebought on October 1, the loss would again be nil. An accidental purchase in a spouse's account need not wait until tax season to be dealt with.
  • Move the loss to the spouse with gains. Paragraph 53(1)(f) adds the denied loss to the spouse's ACB. TaxTips' example: shares with a $10,000 ACB are sold for $6,000, the spouse immediately buys for $6,000, the spouse's ACB becomes $10,000, and after more than 30 days the spouse sells and claims the loss. It does not work in a joint account. The spouse should use their own money; attribution and the general anti-avoidance rule need professional review, and the CRA reserved its position on GAAR in the interpretation above.
  • Same-year offset beats carryback. T4037 states that carrying a net capital loss back reduces taxable income for that year but not net income, so benefits do not change. If you receive the CCB or CGEB, realizing gain and loss in the same year is what lowers net income.
  • Carryback needs no amended return. A 2026 net loss can go to 2023, 2024 or 2025 on Part 5 of Form T1A, and you choose the year. Start with the one taxed at the highest rate.
  • Reconcile average cost across all accounts. Section 47 averages identical properties, across brokers. A statement shows only that account's cost, and the T5008 figure is not your tax figure.

5. Common mistakes

  • "Double up first, sell the old lot later." U.S. investors can identify lots; Canadians cannot. Hold 100 shares at an ACB of $140 with the price at $100, buy another 100 on November 27 (settling November 30), and you are indeed outside the zone. But the average cost falls to $120, so selling 100 on December 30 yields a $2,000 loss. The other $2,000 stays in the remaining shares.
  • "I never bought back." CIBC points out that buying and then selling part of the position inside the zone triggers the rule even with no later purchase. Buy 100 on November 20, sell 25 on December 1 at a $1,000 loss, and the formula denies all of it.
  • "A CDR is a different security." BNN Bloomberg warns that a CDR is a direct interest in the underlying shares and can be identical property. ETFs tracking different indexes usually are not.
  • "All currency losses on U.S. cash are deductible." Subsection 39(1.1) applies a $200 threshold to an individual's net gain or loss on foreign cash; only the excess counts.
  • "Departure losses are caught by the 61-day rule." Paragraph (c) of the definition excludes deemed dispositions under section 128.1, and T4037 lists becoming or ceasing to be resident as an exception.

Frequently asked questions

Is there any point without capital gains?

Capital losses do not offset wages or interest. A net loss can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely and claimed on line 25300.

Does this apply to crypto?

Section 54 refers to property, not only shares. If you report on capital account, the 61 days apply. Exchange settlement conventions do not, so use the actual time of disposition.

I am also a U.S. taxpayer. What changes?

Basis, lot identification and the consequences of a repurchase differ between the two countries, and the Canadian step-up on arrival does not change U.S. basis. Keep separate records and consult a cross-border CPA.

Reminder: rely on the CRA and Justice Canada websites. For spousal transfers, cross-border status or large losses, consult a CPA or tax lawyer.
