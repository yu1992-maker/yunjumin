U.S.–China Treaty Article 20: The $5,000 Student Exemption That Survives Residency

Nov 21, 20265 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Article 20 exempts $5,000 of wages plus remittances and grants, and still applies after year five. Includes a $60,000 OPT example.

Article 20 of the U.S.–China income tax treaty exempts a student who was a PRC resident on arrival from federal income tax on up to $5,000 of U.S. earnings per tax year, plus remittances from abroad and qualifying grants, for as long as is reasonably necessary to finish the program, not for five years. Less widely known: the benefit survives the switch to resident-alien status after five calendar years, because paragraph 2 of the April 30, 1984 Protocol carves it out of the saving clause.

Sources: IRS Publication 901 (U.S. Tax Treaties), Publication 519 (U.S. Tax Guide for Aliens, 2025 edition), the IRS pages for Forms 8843, 8233 and 8833, and the IRS release on 2026 inflation adjustments; checked on November 21, 2026.

1. What Article 20 actually exempts

According to Publication 901, a student, business apprentice or trainee who was a resident of the People's Republic of China on the date of arrival and is in the U.S. solely for education, training or special technical experience is exempt on three kinds of income:

IncomeExemptionTypical case
Payments from abroad for maintenance, education, study, research or trainingNo capLiving money sent by parents
Grants or awards from governmental, scientific, educational or tax-exempt bodiesNo capGovernment study grants; taxable portion of a scholarship
Pay for personal services performed in the U.S.Up to $5,000 per tax yearTA/RA stipends, CPT/OPT wages

Publication 901 limits the benefit to the time reasonably necessary to complete the education or training, and states that the treaty does not apply to Hong Kong.

Publication 519's own example: a Chinese citizen who entered on January 1, 2021 as a nonresident student became a resident on January 1, 2026, yet Article 20 still applies because of the saving-clause exception in paragraph 2 of the Protocol.

3. Worked example: $60,000 of OPT wages in year four versus year six

Assume a single Chinese F-1 student earns $60,000 on OPT in 2026 and has no other income; federal tax only. 2026 brackets and the standard deduction come from the IRS inflation release: 10% up to $12,400, 12% up to $50,400, 22% above that; single standard deduction $16,100.

Scenario A: $12,400 × 10% + $38,000 × 12% + $4,600 × 22% = $6,812. Scenario B: $12,400 × 10% + $26,500 × 12% = $4,420; without the treaty, taxable income would be $43,900 and tax $5,020, so the treaty saves $600.

The swing comes mostly from FICA. Publication 519 says services performed by F, J, M or Q nonresidents to carry out the purpose of their admission (generally on-campus work, practical training and economic-hardship employment) are not subject to Social Security and Medicare withholding; once the person is a resident alien, withholding applies even though the visa class is unchanged.

4. Pro tips

  • File Form 8233 with your employer at the start. While you are a nonresident, Form 8233 can stop withholding on the treaty-exempt amount. Appendix B of Publication 519 supplies the statement Chinese students attach, including the arrival date and the reasonably-necessary-period language, so the $5,000 need not wait for a refund.
  • As a resident, back it out on Schedule 1, line 8z. Where the W-2 puts all wages on line 1a, Publication 519 directs you to enter the exempt amount in parentheses on Schedule 1 line 8z, labelled Exempt income, with the treaty country and article.
  • Form 8833 is usually not required. Publication 519's exceptions cover treaty claims for income of students, trainees and teachers, including taxable scholarships. Where 8833 is required and omitted, the penalty is $1,000 per failure.
  • The arrival date matters. Exempt years count on an any-part-of-the-year basis, so arriving in late December uses up a full calendar year.
  • Mind the sequence with Article 19 (teachers and researchers). The Chinese researcher statement in Publication 519 requires that no exemption was previously claimed as a teacher, researcher or student. On duration, Publication 901 says a total of not more than 3 years, while the Publication 519 appendix statement says an aggregate of 2 years. The two IRS publications differ; check the current form instructions and take professional advice.

5. Common misconceptions

  • The $5,000 lasts only five years. Five years is the exempt-individual residency rule; Article 20 runs for the time reasonably necessary to finish.
  • Residents cannot use treaties. True for most treaties, but Publication 519 uses China as its example of a saving-clause exception available to all resident aliens.
  • No income, no filing. Anyone excluding days must file a complete Form 8843; if no return is due, mail it to Austin, TX 73301-0215.
  • Exempt income never appears on the return. Income reported on a W-2, 1042-S or 1099 is reported first and then subtracted.
  • Hong Kong residents qualify. Publication 901 says the treaty does not apply to Hong Kong.

6. Deadlines

Under Publication 519, calendar-year nonresidents with wages subject to withholding file Form 1040-NR by April 15 of the following year; those without such wages file by June 15. For tax year 2026 that means April 15, 2027 or June 15, 2027. Form 8843 shares the 1040-NR due date.

FAQ

Can I keep Article 20 after moving from F-1 to H-1B?

Publication 901 requires presence solely for study or training; an employment-based status usually does not meet that test. Facts matter, so consult a CPA.

Is a Chinese government scholarship taxable?

Grants from governmental or educational bodies fall within Article 20's second category and are exempt, but if they appear on a 1042-S or 1098-T, report them and then back them out as Publication 519 describes.

Do states follow the treaty?

This article covers federal tax only. State conformity to federal treaties varies; check your state tax agency.
