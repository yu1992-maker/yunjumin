Does a New Mexico LLC Need Liability Insurance? A Guide for Small Web and App Businesses

Nov 8, 20263 min readYunjumin Forum editors

An LLC is not insurance. General liability versus tech E&O, indicative premiums, and when you effectively have to buy.

if you build websites or apps for clients, insurance is advisable, and the first policy to consider is technology E&O (professional liability), not general liability. We found no New Mexico rule requiring ordinary LLCs to carry it. An LLC shields part of your personal exposure and does not pay for the business's own losses. If you only run your own products, with no client contracts and little revenue, you can wait, provided you know the risk.

What an LLC does and does not protect

An LLC separates company debts and lawsuits from personal assets, but the sources agree that the barrier has limits:

  • Personal guarantees. If you guaranteed a company loan or contract, your assets may still be pursued.
  • Commingling. Mixed accounts, missing records or severe undercapitalization may lead a court to disregard the LLC, known as piercing the veil.
  • It is not insurance. It pays neither client claims nor the company's own losses.

Several formation-service sites put New Mexico's filing fee at a one-time $50, with no annual report or annual fee to the Secretary of State. We did not check this against the state's own page; confirm it there.

Three kinds of cover

TypeWhat it coversFor a small web or app business
General liability (GL)Third-party bodily injury and property damageLower risk if you work from home and rarely meet clients
Professional liability / tech E&OClient losses from errors, omissions or alleged negligenceMost relevant: a bug, a late launch, a client who says you cost them business
CyberBreaches, ransomware, unauthorized access and response costsConsider it if you handle client or user data

Sources differ: some say tech E&O bundles E&O and cyber, others treat cyber as separate. Have the broker confirm in writing whether data breaches are covered.

Indicative cost

These figures come from brokers' websites on differing bases. Quotes govern.

  • Web design firms: TechInsurance cites an average of about $68 a month, roughly $810 a year.
  • Software developers: TechInsurance cites about $83 a month, roughly $990 a year; Insureon cites an average of $111 a month.
  • Technology as a whole: Insureon cites annual premiums from about $500 to more than $9,000.

Limits, services, claims history and client size drive the price. A solo operator with a few clients usually pays less than a team serving large ones, and an E&O and cyber bundle can cost less than two policies.

When you effectively have to buy

  1. A client contract requires it. Business clients often ask for a certificate of insurance.
  2. Projects are large, and so are potential claims.
  3. You touch client data, payments or login systems.
  4. You have personal guarantees or significant family assets and could not absorb legal fees.

When you can wait

  • You run only your own products, with no outside clients or contracts.
  • Projects are small and prepaid, with liability caps and disclaimers in the contract.
  • You have no revenue yet. Put contracts, separate accounts and records in order, then buy with the first large client.

Four things to do with or without insurance

  1. Keep a separate company bank account.
  2. Sign a written contract for each project covering scope, acceptance, a liability cap and no guarantee of results.
  3. Retain communications and delivery records.
  4. Do not casually guarantee company contracts or loans.

How to buy

  1. List your services (development, design, maintenance, hosting) and whether you process user data.
  2. Get quotes from two or three brokers or insurers, such as The Hartford, Hiscox or Insureon, comparing limits, deductibles and cyber cover.
  3. Ask about the retroactive date on claims-made policies: claims made after cancellation may not be paid.
  4. Send the certificate to clients who require it.

FAQ

Does New Mexico require LLCs to carry liability insurance?

We found no such rule for ordinary technology LLCs. Employers face other requirements such as workers' compensation; check with the state.

If I have an LLC, do I still need insurance?

They do different things. The LLC separates personal and company liability; if the company is sued, it still bears the fees and damages.

Is one of GL or E&O enough?

For web and app work the main risk sits under E&O. GL mainly covers injury and property damage, and matters more with an office or visiting clients.
