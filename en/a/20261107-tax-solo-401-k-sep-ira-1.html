Solo 401(k) or SEP IRA: 2026 Limits and Deadlines

Nov 7, 20263 min readYunjumin Forum editors

2026 contribution limits, set-up and funding deadlines compared, with a year-end checklist for the self-employed.

If income is moderate and you want to save more, choose a Solo 401(k), which combines an employee part ($24,500 for 2026) with an employer part. If income is high and simplicity matters, a SEP IRA is easier to open but has only the employer part. To use a Solo 401(k) against 2026 tax, make the employee deferral election by December 31 and do not leave it to the final week.

2026 limits

From the IRS (Notice 2025-67 and the 401(k) limits page):

Item2026
Employee elective deferral$24,500
Catch-up, age 50 and over$8,000
Catch-up, ages 60 to 63$11,250
Overall limit, excluding catch-up$72,000
Overall limit, including catch-up$80,000; $83,250 at ages 60 to 63
Compensation cap$360,000

The SEP limit is the lesser of 25% of compensation and a dollar cap. IRS Publication 560 (2025) states $70,000. The $72,000 figure for 2026 is inferred from the 415(c) limit; we did not find it on the SEP page itself, so check the latest IRS text. A SEP has no employee part and no catch-up.

Calculating the employer part

The IRS defines a self-employed person's compensation as net earnings less half of self-employment tax and less contributions for yourself, so the effective rate is about 20%, not 25%. Use the tables or worksheets in chapter 5 of Publication 560.

Our estimate: Schedule C profit of $100,000. Self-employment tax is about $14,130, half is $7,065, and the base is about $92,935. A SEP allows about $18,587; a Solo 401(k) about $24,500 + $18,587 = $43,087 (under age 50).

The gap is widest at lower incomes. On $40,000 of profit a SEP allows a little over $7,000, while the Solo 401(k) employee part permits considerably more, limited by income.

Deadlines, and where sources differ

  • SEP IRA: Publication 560 allows set-up and contribution by the return due date, including extensions.
  • Solo 401(k) employee part: Publication 560 requires the election by year end; the money may follow by the return due date, including extensions.
  • Opening a Solo 401(k) after year end: Under SECURE 2.0, Publication 560 says that from 2023 a sole proprietor with no employees may establish a 401(k) after the year ends, but by the return due date without extensions.
  • Accounts differ: some private sites say it must be opened by December 31; others say a later plan may take employee deferrals, or only the employer part. Follow Publication 560 and ask your CPA and broker about deferrals.

Year-end checklist

  1. Estimate 2026 net profit and how much you can contribute.
  2. If you want the employee part, open a Solo 401(k) with a broker and elect a deferral rate by December 31.
  3. If you prefer simplicity or have high income, use Form 5305-SEP and open at filing time.
  4. Apply for an EIN first if one is needed (employees or an entity).
  5. Fund by the 2027 filing deadline, or the extended date if you extend.
  6. At filing, sole proprietors and partners deduct their own contributions on Schedule 1 (Form 1040) under Publication 560; contributions for employees go on Schedule C.
  7. Once Solo 401(k) assets reach $250,000, file Form 5500-EZ each year.

Points to watch

  • The employee deferral limit is per person, not per plan. With another employer's 401(k), the two together cannot exceed $24,500.
  • Hiring eligible staff mid-year brings them into the plan and raises cost.
  • An extension of the return does not extend the personal IRA contribution deadline.

FAQ

One W-2 job and a small side business: which?

Usually the Solo 401(k) is more flexible, but the deferral limit is shared. If the main job already uses the $24,500, the side business can only take employer contributions and a SEP is simpler.

Can I still contribute for 2025?

The original April 15 deadline has passed and the extended date is generally October 15, so in November it is normally too late. Check your own extension.

Can I have both?

Yes, but employer contributions share one overall limit, and most people have no need.
