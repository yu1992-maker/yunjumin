UK Child Benefit: the £60,000 Charge and the NRPF Line

Nov 25, 20266 min readYunjumin Forum editors

£2,337 a year for two children, clawed back from £60,000 to £80,000. FIG income counts, and high earners should still register.

UK Child Benefit for 2026-27 is £27.05 a week for the eldest child and £17.90 for each other, or £2,337.40 a year for two. If either partner's adjusted net income exceeds £60,000, 1% is charged back for every £200 above it, reaching 100% at £80,000. Two lines catch newcomers. Someone whose visa carries a no recourse to public funds (NRPF) condition usually cannot claim. And an eligible family earning more than £80,000 should still register a claim without payment, to protect National Insurance credits.

Sources: GOV.UK guidance on Child Benefit and the High Income Child Benefit Charge (HICBC), HMRC's rates table updated November 10, 2026, HMRC guidance on adjusted net income, the Home Office Public funds guidance, and HMRC's March 30, 2026 notice on replacement credits. Checked November 25, 2026.

1. Immigration status comes before income

For people moving from abroad GOV.UK adds two conditions: the UK must be your main home and you must have the right to reside. In most cases the child must live with you, and a family from outside the EU, Switzerland, Norway, Iceland or Liechtenstein will usually not qualify for a child living elsewhere.

The Home Office guidance says a person is not in breach of NRPF where the partner is entitled in their own right, is the main applicant or recipient, and the restricted person is merely included for a joint assessment. Who signs as claimant therefore matters. The agreement-country wording is also worth noting: the Home Office refers to nationals of those countries or people who have come to live in the UK from them, while the public GOV.UK page simply says "from". China is not listed. A family that previously lived in Canada could raise the point with the Child Benefit Office, but should not assume the answer.

2. How the charge works

The test is individual, not household. If both partners exceed £60,000, the one with the higher income pays. A couple earning £59,000 each keeps everything; a single-earner couple on £80,000 keeps nothing.

Expressed as a marginal rate, between £60,000 and £80,000 a two-child family loses about £11.69 for each extra £100. Added to 40% income tax and 2% National Insurance, that is roughly 53.7% in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. With one child it is about 49.0%, with three about 58.3%. Scottish rates differ.

HMRC's provisional 2027-28 rates are £28.00 and £18.55, or £2,420.60 a year for two children. GOV.UK's current pages show no change to the £60,000 and £80,000 lines.

3. Adjusted net income has a trap for new arrivals

HMRC's list includes pay and benefits in kind, self-employed profit, savings interest, dividends, some rent and foreign income, "including income where a claim under the foreign income and gains regime has been made". The FIG regime can exempt foreign income from UK tax for a newcomer's first four years. It does not remove that income from the HICBC test.

Case one. Second year in the UK, £55,000 salary, £10,000 of foreign dividends, FIG claimed. Adjusted net income is £65,000, the charge is 25%, and a two-child family owes about £584.

Case two. £66,000 salary and nothing else. Paying £4,800 net into a relief-at-source personal pension by April 5, 2027 is grossed up to £6,000, bringing adjusted net income to £60,000. That saves about £701 of charge and allows £1,200 of further higher-rate relief, so £6,000 in the pension costs about £2,899. The money is locked away, so liquidity matters.

4. Advanced points

  1. Do not opt out between £60,000 and £80,000. GOV.UK confirms the charge never exceeds the benefit. Taking payment and repaying part always leaves something.
  2. Above £80,000, claim but decline payment. While the child is under 12 the registered person gets Class 3 credits automatically, paid or not, and the child usually receives an NI number shortly before 16. The full new State Pension is £241.30 a week for 35 qualifying years, so one year is worth about £6.89 a week, or £358 a year.
  3. Let the partner without an NI record claim. Credits follow the claimant. They can be transferred between partners on application, but registering correctly at the outset is simpler.
  4. Missed October 5? PAYE may still be open. For 2025-26, Self Assessment registration was due by October 5, 2026. GOV.UK says employees with no other reason to file may instead pay through their tax code until January 31, 2027. After that only Self Assessment remains.
  5. Payments can be stopped and restarted. The charge depends on income for the whole tax year. If income falls short of expectations, a restart may bring some backdated payment, and the first payment can take up to 28 days.
  6. Missed credits can be recovered from April 2027. Parents eligible from January 7, 2013 who did not claim for a child under 12 may apply when the replacement credits service opens. It was due in April 2026 and has slipped a year. Those reaching State Pension age before April 6, 2027 who lose out may complain quoting RCPC.

6. Common misconceptions

  • "It is capped at two children." GOV.UK says there is no limit.
  • "High earners need not claim." They lose credits, and backdating is only three months.
  • "I do not receive it, so the charge is not mine." The higher earner pays even if the partner receives it, married or not. HMRC will answer yes or no to a written question about a partner.
  • "NRPF only covers unemployment support." Child Benefit is on the list of public funds, and claiming wrongly can breach visa conditions.

FAQ

Can I claim for past years once I get ILR?

No. Backdating is limited to three months, and you must have been eligible in that period.

What if a partner moves in mid-year?

The charge runs from the date of moving in until permanent separation or the end of payments. Short absences do not count as separation.

With PAYE, do I still file a return?

Not unless there is another reason to file, such as self-employment or savings and investment income that requires one.
