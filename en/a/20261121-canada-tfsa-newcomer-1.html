Canada's TFSA for Newcomers: Room Starts the Year You Arrive, and Excess Costs 1% a Month

Nov 21, 20265 min readYunjumin Forum editors

Newcomers get TFSA room from their year of residency: $7,000 in 2026, not pro-rated. Excess is taxed at 1% a month, and withdrawals return as room only the next 1 January.

a newcomer's TFSA room starts in the year they become a Canadian tax resident, provided they are 18 or older. They receive the full annual limit for that year, $7,000 in 2026, with no pro-rating for the month of arrival. The roughly $109,000 accumulated since 2009 does not apply to them. Over-contributions are taxed at 1% of the highest excess in each month, so even a single day counts as a full month. Money withdrawn during the year only becomes room again on 1 January of the following year.

1. How room is calculated: the formula and the annual limits

The CRA's formula: the current year's dollar limit, plus unused room from previous years, plus withdrawals made in the previous year, minus contributions already made this year, equals your available room. Qualifying transfers, exempt contributions and specified distributions do not affect room. All your TFSAs share one limit.

These limits add up to $109,000, but only for someone who has been resident and aged 18 or over since 2009. The CRA states that new residents start accumulating room on the day they become resident, and that room begins in the year of residency rather than the year they turned 18. The limit is indexed to inflation, rounded to the nearest $500 and added every 1 January.

3. Worked example: a newcomer arriving in June 2026

Suppose A, aged 30, becomes a Canadian tax resident in June 2026, receives a SIN and opens a TFSA.

  • Room for 2026: $7,000. The CRA says a person resident for part of the year receives the full annual dollar limit for that year.
  • Scenario 1: misreading cumulative room. A bank web page says cumulative room since 2009 is $109,000, and A deposits $50,000 in October. The $43,000 excess costs $430 a month. By the end of December that is three months, or $1,290, regardless of investment performance.
  • Scenario 2: withdrawing and putting it back. A contributes the full $7,000 in July, withdraws $3,000 in September for an emergency and redeposits $3,000 in November. Because the withdrawal is not added back until 1 January 2027, the November deposit is a $3,000 excess. Left until year-end, the tax is $3,000 × 1% × 2 months = $60.

The CRA's own example shows how unforgiving the monthly rule is. Rosanna went $2,100 over on 30 October 2026. Left in place to year-end, the tax is $2,100 × 1% × 3 months = $63. Even if she had withdrawn the $2,100 on 31 October, she would still owe $21 for October. Remove an excess the moment you find it, but expect that month's 1% to apply regardless.

4. Pro tips

  • If you qualify as a first-time buyer, open an FHSA early. The CRA gives $8,000 of participation room in the year you open your first FHSA, with an annual carry-forward capped at $8,000 and a lifetime limit of $40,000. FHSA room starts when you open the account, not when you land, so a year's delay costs a year of carry-forward. You must file Schedule 15 for the year you open it, even if you contribute nothing.
  • Use your spouse's room. The CRA allows you to give money to a spouse or common-law partner to contribute to their own TFSA, and neither the gift nor its income is attributed back to you. A couple landing together can shelter $14,000 in year one.
  • Move between issuers by direct transfer. A transfer arranged by the receiving institution does not use room. Withdrawing and recontributing yourself can create an excess in the same year.
  • Leave a margin with foreign-currency deposits. The CRA converts them to Canadian dollars at the exchange rate on the transaction date, so a deposit meant to fill your room exactly can end up over the limit.
  • Never move RRSP assets into a TFSA. The transfer is treated as an RRSP withdrawal at fair market value and is taxable income that year.
  • Do not contribute shares that are down in kind. An in-kind contribution is a deemed disposition at fair market value. Gains must be reported, but losses cannot be claimed.

5. Common misconceptions

  • "Newcomers can catch up on room back to 2009." Room starts in the year you become resident.
  • "The figure in my CRA account is my live room." The CRA explicitly recommends calculating room from your own records. Account data is refreshed only once a year, in spring.
  • "I can top up after an investment loss." Losses do not add room, and gains do not reduce it.
  • "I must close my TFSA when I leave Canada." You can keep it, and income inside is not taxed in Canada, though your new country may tax it. Every contribution made while non-resident attracts the 1% monthly tax.
  • "A TFSA is tax-free for everyone." US citizens and green card holders face separate US reporting issues; see our article on how US persons report RRSPs and TFSAs.

FAQ

Can I open a TFSA before I receive my SIN?

No. The CRA lists a valid SIN as a condition. Room accrues from the year of residency, so opening a few weeks later does not reduce that year's room.

If I land in December, do I still get $7,000 for that year?

Yes. The CRA says anyone resident for part of the year receives that year's dollar limit, and unused room carries forward.

What should I do if I have over-contributed?

Withdraw the excess immediately to stop the monthly tax. The CRA's page "If you over-contribute to a TFSA" explains how the tax is reported. For large amounts or non-resident contributions, use an accountant.
