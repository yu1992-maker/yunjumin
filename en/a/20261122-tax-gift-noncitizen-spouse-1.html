Gifts to a non-citizen spouse: the $194,000 cap and Form 709

Nov 22, 20264 min readYunjumin Forum editors

A green card spouse gets no unlimited marital deduction: the cap is $194,000 for 2026 and $201,000 for 2027, with Form 709 above it.

Gifts between spouses who are US citizens qualify for the unlimited marital deduction. If the receiving spouse is not a US citizen, green card holders included, that deduction disappears and is replaced by an annual cap: $194,000 for 2026 and $201,000 for 2027. Anything above the cap must be reported by the donor on Form 709 by 15 April of the following year. Tax is rarely due, but the filing is mandatory. Splitting a transfer across two calendar years is the simplest compliant answer.

Sources: IRS news releases IR-2025-103 (2026 inflation adjustments) and IR-2026-133 (5 November 2026, 2027 adjustments, Revenue Procedure 2026-35); the 2025 Instructions for Form 709; and the IRS gift tax FAQs for residents and for nonresidents not citizens. Checked on 22 November 2026.

1. The numbers, 2025 to 2027

Item202520262027
Annual exclusion per donee$19,000$19,000$20,000
Annual exclusion for a non-citizen spouse$190,000$194,000$201,000
Basic exclusion amount (estate and gift)$13,990,000$15,000,000$15,480,000

The 2026 figures come from IR-2025-103 and the 2027 figures from IR-2026-133. The 2025 Form 709 instructions still print $190,000, which applies to 2025 gifts; gifts made in 2026 and reported in 2027 use $194,000.

What decides the regime is the recipient spouse's citizenship, not residency. Green card holders and spouses on H-1B or F-1 status are all non-citizens. The donor's own status decides the form: US citizens and residents file Form 709, while nonresident non-citizen donors are subject to gift tax only on US-situated real and tangible property and file Form 709-NA.

2. Worked example: a down payment in the spouse's name

Assume the husband holds a green card and the wife is on an H-1B. They buy a home in Florida; in June 2026 he moves $250,000 from his own account to hers and the property is titled in her name.

  • Non-citizen spouse exclusion: $194,000 is excluded; the remaining $56,000 is a reportable gift.
  • Filing: he must file a 2026 Form 709 by 15 April 2027. The $56,000 uses part of his lifetime exclusion, leaving about $14,944,000. No tax is payable.
  • Had the wife been a US citizen: the unlimited marital deduction would apply and no return would be needed.

If the same $250,000 were split into $194,000 in December 2026 and $56,000 in January 2027, each year would stay within its cap. Provided both are present-interest gifts, no Form 709 would be required for either year. The Form 709 instructions say gifts to a non-citizen spouse need not be reported on Schedule A if all are present interests, the annual total is within the cap, and the amount above the ordinary exclusion would have qualified for the marital deduction had the spouse been a citizen.

3. Pro tips

  1. Straddle the year end. One transfer in December and one in January can move $395,000 ($194,000 + $201,000) without a return, as long as each gift genuinely belongs to the spouse with immediate use.
  2. Pay tuition and medical bills directly. The Form 709 instructions treat qualifying tuition and medical expenses paid directly to the institution or provider as non-taxable transfers that do not use the annual cap. Pay a spouse's MBA fees to the school, not to the spouse.
  3. Know when a joint account gift happens. Opening a joint bank account is not itself a gift; the instructions say the gift occurs when the other owner withdraws funds for their own benefit, in the amount withdrawn.
  4. Gifts to parents and the split-gift rule. Couples who are both US citizens or residents can elect gift splitting, $38,000 per donee in 2026. The IRS FAQ says splitting is unavailable if either spouse is a nonresident non-citizen; each must then file separately.
  5. Keep the paper trail. Retain transfer records, title documents and copies of any Form 709. They matter for basis when the home is sold and for estate planning if the spouse later naturalises.

4. Common misconceptions

  • "A green card spouse is treated like a citizen." The marital deduction follows citizenship; green card holders remain subject to the $194,000 cap.
  • "Gift tax only matters to the wealthy." Few people pay it, but exceeding the cap creates a filing obligation. Filing and paying are different things.
  • "Spouses can file Form 709 jointly." Form 709 is an individual return; there is no joint version.
  • "This is the same as Form 3520." Form 3520 is filed by a US recipient of more than $100,000 in gifts from a foreign individual. Form 709 is filed by a US donor. Transfers between spouses normally involve only the latter.
  • "A trust solves it." Future-interest gifts to a non-citizen spouse do not qualify for the annual exclusion, and any such gift means every gift to the spouse that year must be reported.

FAQ

Can the deadline be extended?

Form 709 is generally filed between 1 January and 15 April of the year after the gift. The instructions say an income tax extension on Form 4868 also extends Form 709; Form 8892 can be used on its own.

If my spouse later naturalises, must earlier gifts be reported?

Earlier gifts follow the rules in force at the time; those within the cap need no return. Gifts after naturalisation qualify for the unlimited marital deduction. Years in which the cap was exceeded without a return should be addressed promptly with professional advice.

I am a nonresident non-citizen. Do transfers to my spouse in the US need reporting?

The IRS nonresident FAQ says such donors are subject to gift tax only on US-situated real and tangible property, and intangibles are generally outside its scope. How cash and bank deposits are characterised is interpreted differently by different advisers, so take legal advice before large transfers.

Verify on IRS.gov. For trusts, jointly held property or cross-border estate planning, consult a CPA or attorney.
